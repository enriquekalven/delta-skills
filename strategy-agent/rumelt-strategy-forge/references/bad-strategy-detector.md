# Bad Strategy Detector
> Reference: rumelt-strategy-forge | Phase: Quality Assurance | Voice: Ruthlessly Honest

## Overview
This module kills bad strategy before it wastes organizational energy. Rumelt identifies four hallmarks of strategic failure—each predictable, each curable if caught early. Use this detector at *strategy review gates* (after diagnostic phase, before commitment). **No exceptions.**

---

## Hallmark 1: FLUFF
**Definition:** Inflated abstract language masquerading as insight. Buzzwords repackaging the obvious into pseudo-sophistication.

### Diagnostic Severity Framework (0-4)

**Score 4 (Fatal):** Strategy reads like corporate Mad Libs. Substitute random nouns; meaning unchanged.
- Questions:
  1. Can a front-line employee read this and know what job she must do differently Monday morning?
  2. Could you remove 40%+ of the words without losing meaning?
  3. Does it define "leverage," "synergy," "ecosystem," "transformation" operationally?
  4. Would an outsider reading this think you're saying something specific or hiding?
  5. Are 3+ sentences just rewording the previous sentence?
  6. Does it pass the "falsifiability test"? Could you prove it wrong?
  7. Count vague intensifiers: "world-class," "best-in-class," "industry-leading," "cutting-edge." >2 = red flag.
  8. Does it identify the actual mechanism of competitive advantage or just say "be better"?

**Score 3 (Serious):** Insight present but buried under padding. Clarity requires excavation.

**Score 2 (Moderate):** Some jargon but mostly defensible. One rewrite needed.

**Score 1 (Minor):** One or two buzzwords; strategic point clear.

**Score 0:** Language matches rigor of thought. Specific. Testable.

### Remediation Steps
1. **The Strikethrough Test:** Take your strategy. Mark every adjective and qualifier. Delete them. Does the core remain? If not, no core exists.
2. **The 12-Year-Old Rule:** Rewrite each sentence so a bright 12-year-old gets it. If you can't, the idea is fuzzy.
3. **Operational Specificity:** Every strategic claim must map to at least one observable action or metric. "Improve customer experience" → "Reduce phone support wait time from 8 min to 2 min."
4. **The Competitor Inversion:** Could your competitor use this exact strategy? If yes, it's not specific enough to be strategic.

### Before/After Examples

**FLUFF Example 1:**
- **Bad:** "Leverage core competencies to drive operational excellence and create synergistic value through integrated customer-centric solutions."
- **Fixed:** "Combine our 15-year supply-chain expertise with direct customer relationships to offer 2-day delivery at competitive price—a capability UPS can't match cheaply."

**FLUFF Example 2:**
- **Bad:** "Transform organizational culture through strategic alignment and world-class talent development initiatives."
- **Fixed:** "Hire 50 engineers from direct competitors over 18 months, each owning a product module. This reduces time-to-market for new features from 9 months to 3."

**FLUFF Example 3:**
- **Bad:** "Establish best-in-class technology infrastructure to enable digital transformation and unlock growth potential."
- **Fixed:** "Build ML pipeline that predicts churn 60 days early; customer success team targets 200 at-risk accounts monthly for retention. Projected revenue impact: $8M annually."

## Hallmark 2: FAILURE TO FACE THE CHALLENGE
**Definition:** Skips honest diagnosis. Jumps to aspirations. Papers over real obstacles with vague language.

### Diagnostic Severity Framework (0-4)

**Score 4 (Fatal):** Strategy avoids naming the core problem. Reads like wish-fulfillment.
- Questions:
  1. Does the strategy explicitly name the single hardest constraint or competitor threat?
  2. Would an outsider with domain knowledge recognize the actual challenge from reading this?
  3. Does it honestly acknowledge what the organization is *bad* at?
  4. Are disagreements about the diagnosis visible, or papered over with consensus language?
  5. Does it explain *why* this challenge exists now (market shift, missed investment, competitive move)?
  6. Could you give this to a skeptical board member and have her believe you understand the problem?
  7. Does it mention resources, capabilities, or market conditions that make the challenge harder?
  8. Is there a "so what?"—consequences if you ignore the challenge?

**Score 3 (Serious):** Problem named but softened. Edges blurred. Severity minimized.

**Score 2 (Moderate):** Diagnosis present and mostly honest. Some avoidance around leadership weakness or market size.

**Score 1 (Minor):** Clear challenge statement; some minor softening.

**Score 0:** Unflinching. Specific threat or bottleneck. Naked truth.

### Remediation Steps
1. **The Hostile Question:** A smart rival reads your strategy. Where would she attack? If you don't mention it, you haven't diagnosed correctly.
2. **Name the Constraint:** "Our challenge is not growth—it's [engineering talent scarcity / logistics cost / regulatory delay]." One sentence. No wiggle room.
3. **Visible Disagreement:** Show what leaders debated. "Sales wanted feature X; we chose Y because [reason]. Both camps understand why."
4. **The Consequentialist Test:** "If we do nothing, [specific bad thing] happens in [timeframe]. This is unacceptable because [business impact]."
5. **Explicitness Audit:** Delete every sentence that could apply to 10 companies in your industry. Keep only diagnosis that applies to *you*.

### Before/After Examples

**FAILURE TO FACE Example 1:**
- **Bad:** "Market conditions are evolving. We must stay agile and innovate faster to remain competitive."
- **Fixed:** "AWS is undercutting our database licensing margins by 40% annually. Our $2B database business has 3 years to transition to SaaS pricing or face margin collapse. This requires building new sales infrastructure and retraining 30% of our account executives."

**FAILURE TO FACE Example 2:**
- **Bad:** "Customer needs are changing. We need to be more responsive and customer-focused."
- **Fixed:** "Three major customers (40% of revenue) threatened to leave if we don't integrate with Salesforce by Q3. We lack CRM expertise in-house; hiring is slow. Decision: partner with Deloitte, or build 8-person CRM team in 12 weeks."

**FAILURE TO FACE Example 3:**
- **Bad:** "Operational efficiency and cost management are strategic priorities."
- **Fixed:** "Our ops team is split across 8 systems with 4x data entry. This costs us $6M annually and causes 15% of invoicing errors. Competitors with unified systems close deals 30% faster. Fix: migrate to Workday, 18-month timeline, accept 2-quarter slowdown."

## Hallmark 3: MISTAKING GOALS FOR STRATEGY
**Definition:** Desired outcomes dressed as strategy. "Grow 20%" is a goal. Strategy is *how* you'll grow.

### Diagnostic Severity Framework (0-4)

**Score 4 (Fatal):** Pure goal statement masquerading as strategy. No mechanisms. No trade-offs.
- Questions:
  1. If you removed the word "strategy," does it still sound like a valid business sentence?
  2. Does it list *what* you want (outcome) or *how* you'll get there (mechanism)?
  3. Are all items financial targets (revenue, margin, share) with no operational methods?
  4. Could a lazy competitor copy this and think they're executing strategy?
  5. Does it describe a sequence of moves or just end-state?
  6. Is there a guiding principle that eliminates certain options?
  7. Does it trade off anything, or try to maximize everything?
  8. If budget were cut 30%, which part would you ruthlessly cut? If unclear, these aren't real priorities.

**Score 3 (Serious):** 50% goals, 50% mechanisms. Vague on trade-offs. Goals outweigh strategy.

**Score 2 (Moderate):** Clear goals + some mechanisms. Strategy visible but secondary.

**Score 1 (Minor):** Goals clearly stated; strategy is primary. Minor emphasis drift.

**Score 0:** Strategy dominates. Goals emerge naturally from strategy choices.

### Remediation Steps
1. **The Substitution Rule:** If you can swap "strategy" for "wish" without changing meaning, it's not strategy—rewrite it.
2. **Mechanism Clarity:** Every strategic claim must have an operational "because."
   - Goal: "Grow revenue 25%"
   - Strategy: "We'll grow revenue 25% *because* we're entering SMB market where we can underprice enterprise vendors by 40%, and our product needs 20% fewer integrations."
3. **The Trade-Off Explicit:** Write down what you're *not* doing. "We will not pursue [market segment / feature / business line] because [reason]." This proves goals have constraints.
4. **Convert Goals to Milestones:** Goals mark finish lines. Break them into quarterly operational milestones tied to strategic moves.

### Before/After Examples

**GOALS NOT STRATEGY Example 1:**
- **Bad:** "Grow to $100M revenue, expand to 3 new markets, improve NPS to 70, reduce churn 15%."
- **Fixed:** "We'll reach $100M by selling to mid-market (vs. enterprise). This requires: (1) product simplification to cut implementation from 6mo to 2mo; (2) hiring sales team focused on contracts <$500K. Markets: UK, Germany, Canada—English-speaking first. Our expansion assumes we can hire 20 sales people/year; if hiring fails, growth caps at $60M."

**GOALS NOT STRATEGY Example 2:**
- **Bad:** "Improve operational efficiency and achieve market leadership in our category."
- **Fixed:** "We'll become category leader by being the only player offering monthly (vs. annual) licensing. This requires building subscription billing infrastructure (6mo, $2M), retraining sales to avoid up-front revenue recognition issues, and targeting companies with <50 employees who churn suppliers frequently. We'll sacrifice enterprise deals to win this segment."

**GOALS NOT STRATEGY Example 3:**
- **Bad:** "Increase profitability, invest in R&D, and strengthen customer relationships."
- **Fixed:** "We'll improve margins from 20% to 28% by cutting our SKU count from 400 to 60. We'll invest savings in 3 core R&D bets: ML personalization, mobile app, API platform. We sacrifice breadth (goodbye low-margin SKUs) to go deep (higher customer stickiness)."

## Hallmark 4: BAD STRATEGIC OBJECTIVES
**Definition:** Objectives either too abstract (blue sky) or undisciplined (laundry list). Unactionable or contradictory.

### Diagnostic Severity Framework (0-4)

**Score 4 (Fatal):** >7 objectives. Mix of vague platitudes and tactical items. No visible priority or trade-offs.
- Questions:
  1. How many objectives? >5 is a warning; >7 is failure.
  2. Can a team lead translate each objective into 2-3 concrete actions in the next 90 days?
  3. Do any two objectives contradict (e.g., "maximize margin" and "gain market share aggressively")?
  4. Could you rank them? If ranking is impossible, there's no strategy—just a list.
  5. Are resources allocated proportionally to objective priority?
  6. Would an outside expert think these are strategic levers or hygiene factors?
  7. Do objectives describe the means (competitive advantage) or the ends (what you want to achieve)?
  8. Is there one objective so abstract it's unfalsifiable?

**Score 3 (Serious):** 5-6 objectives. One or two are wishy-washy. Trade-offs unclear.

**Score 2 (Moderate):** 4 objectives. Mostly clear. One needs tightening.

**Score 1 (Minor):** 3-4 objectives. All defensible. Minor clarity issue.

**Score 0:** 2-4 objectives. Testable. Ranked. Integrated. Mutually reinforcing.

### Remediation Steps
1. **The Five-Objective Max:** Cut ruthlessly. If you can't rank five items, you don't understand your strategy.
2. **The Feasibility Gut-Check:** Given current team, budget, and market conditions, can you realistically hit all objectives in 2 years? If gut says no, cut one.
3. **The Mechanism Requirement:** Each objective must link to one of your strategic pillars (competitive advantage, market choice, or guiding policy).
4. **Trade-Off Visibility:** For each objective, write one sentence: "We chose X over Y because [competitive advantage]."
5. **The Reverse-Engineer Test:** Start with objectives. Can someone unfamiliar with your strategy reverse-engineer your market choice and competitive advantage? If not, objectives are wrong.

### Before/After Examples

**BAD OBJECTIVES Example 1:**
- **Bad:** "Expand market presence, improve operational efficiency, strengthen customer engagement, accelerate innovation, enhance employee development, drive profitability, build strategic partnerships."
- **Fixed:** "(1) Build AI-powered recommendation engine—our defensible edge vs. Amazon. (2) Sell to SMB (not enterprise)—unit economics work at $50K/year contract. (3) Hire 30 ML engineers—none available; this is the constraint."

**BAD OBJECTIVES Example 2:**
- **Bad:** "Achieve 50% YoY revenue growth, improve customer satisfaction, reduce costs, expand product line, enter adjacent markets."
- **Fixed:** "(1) Achieve 35% YoY growth via land-and-expand (customers starting at $10K, expanding to $100K over 3 years). (2) This requires 80% NPS; today we're at 65. (3) Reduce product to 4 core modules (cut 60% of SKU complexity), reinvest savings into NPS improvements."

**BAD OBJECTIVES Example 3:**
- **Bad:** "Be world-class, innovate continuously, maximize shareholder value, improve culture, dominate market segment."
- **Fixed:** "(1) Own the mid-market HR software segment—be top 3 by revenue. (2) Compete on speed: 8-week implementation vs. 16-week for ADP. (3) Hire 100 onboarding specialists; they're the moat."

## The Fluff-O-Meter
### Banned Phrases → Remediation Rewrites

| **Banned Phrase** | **Why It's Poison** | **Replacement** |
|---|---|---|
| "Leverage our core competencies" | Vague virtue. Doesn't say which competencies or how. | "Use our 12-year track record in [specific domain] to undercut competitors 35%." |
| "Synergy" | Means nothing. Every merger claims it. | "Eliminate 40 FTE of duplicate finance/HR functions; redeploy to sales." |
| "Create value" | For whom? How much? | "Increase customer lifetime value from $50K to $120K by bundling services." |
| "World-class" | Unverifiable. Lazy. | "Top quartile in [specific metric] among [competitor set]." |
| "Digital transformation" | Covers sins. Hides real change. | "Migrate 80% of transactions to API; eliminate phone support by 2027." |
| "Agile" | Everybody claims it. Says nothing. | "Ship features in 2-week sprints; measure usability with 100 customers/sprint." |
| "Enhance stakeholder value" | Meaningless soft-speak. | "Increase ROIC from 8% to 15% in 24 months." |
| "Improve customer experience" | So vague it's useless. | "Reduce complaint resolution time from 7 days to 24 hours." |
| "Best-in-class" | Signifies nothing. Unfalsifiable. | "Achieve <2% defect rate; better than Competitor A (4%), Competitor B (3%)." |
| "Transformational" | Red alert. Usually hides weak idea. | "Change business model from licensing to SaaS; $80M revenue impact by Y3." |
| "Unlock potential" | Potential exists; you're saying nothing. | "Sell existing software to 5 new verticals; $25M new revenue/year." |
| "Optimize" | For what? Revenue? Margin? Speed? | "Reduce production cost 20% via [specific automation / outsourcing]." |
| "Ecosystem approach" | Trendy. Vague. Dangerous. | "Open our API to 3 ecosystem partners; they drive 15% of new revenue." |
| "Mission-driven" | Every nonprofit and startup claims this. | "Measurable goal: serve 10M underbanked customers by 2030." |
| "Innovation" | Everyone innovates. Worthless. | "Dedicate 20% of engineering to bets; require $5M+ upside if successful." |
| "Scalable" | Overused. Mostly means "built on cloud." | "Architecture supports 10M concurrent users; tested to 50M. Today at 5M." |
| "Disruption" | Predict the future; hard. | "Target niche where customer pain is $500K+/yr but no solution exists." |
| "Holistic approach" | Makes you sound thoughtful. Is meaningless. | "Integrate sales, marketing, and customer success into unified revenue team." |
| "Cutting-edge" | Implies technical sophistication. Usually doesn't exist. | "Use [specific technology] before competitors adopt it; 18-month window." |
| "Empower employees" | Sounds good. Meaningless. | "Delegate P&L to 8 product teams; each has hiring and budget autonomy." |

## Organizational Psychology: Why Bad Strategy Happens (& How to Overcome It)

### Root Cause 1: Leadership Avoidance
**The Problem:** CEOs avoid naming hard constraints or weakness because it invites challenge. Safer to hide behind jargon.
- Manifests in: Fluff, Failure to Face the Challenge
- **Antidote:** Reward (privately) the leader who names the problem first. Make naming problems a status signal, not weakness. Board composition: add directors who've built things and know cost of soft language. Give them veto power over strategy docs with >3 vague adjectives.

### Root Cause 2: Consensus-Seeking
**The Problem:** Leadership teams avoid disagreement by using abstract language everyone can project onto.
- Manifests in: Fluff, Bad Objectives
- **Antidote:** Require explicit disagreements in the strategy document. "Finance wanted price hikes; Sales wanted volume. We chose volume because [reason]." Make visible disagreement a sign of rigor, not dysfunction.

### Root Cause 3: Fear of Constraint
**The Problem:** Organizations fear saying "no" to markets, products, features. Everyone assumes big markets = big opportunity. Ignoring constraints makes them go away (it doesn't).
- Manifests in: Bad Strategic Objectives (laundry list), Mistaking Goals for Strategy
- **Antidote:** Reframe constraints as strategic assets. "We can't serve enterprise because we lack sales infrastructure. Instead, we own SMB." Make trade-offs visible. Ask: "What are we proudly not doing?"

### Root Cause 4: Impatience
**The Problem:** Teams rush strategy to move to "execution." Strategy becomes a check-box, not thinking tool.
- Manifests in: All four hallmarks
- **Antidote:** Build time into process. 4-week diagnostic phase minimum. Require written hypotheses. Stage strategy reviews (diagnostic → guiding policy → objectives → final). Slow down to speed up.

### Root Cause 5: Board/Investor Pressure
**The Problem:** VCs and boards demand optimistic projections. Managers deliver vague, aspirational strategy to justify growth targets no one believes in.
- Manifests in: Mistaking Goals for Strategy, Fluff
- **Antidote:** Separate strategy from projections. Strategy is mechanism. Projections are conditional on mechanism working. Present both: "If we execute this strategy flawlessly, revenue could reach $X. Today, we're at Y. Here's the gap."

## Scoring System: The Bad Strategy Scorecard

### How to Score
1. Score each hallmark 0-4 using the Diagnostic Severity Framework above.
2. Sum the four hallmarks: **Total Score /16**
3. Record date and review name.
4. **Note:** Higher scores = worse strategy. Aim low.

### Scoring Thresholds & Action

| **Total Score** | **Verdict** | **Action** |
|---|---|---|
| 0-3 | **PROCEED** | Strategy is sound. Move to execution. Quarterly review gates only. |
| 4-7 | **REVISE** | Rework 1-2 hallmarks. Add clarity. Resubmit in 2 weeks. |
| 8-12 | **DEEP REVISE** | Strategy fundamentally weak. Revisit diagnostic phase. Rewrite from scratch. 4-week cycle. |
| 13-16 | **RESTART** | Restart strategy process. This strategy will burn organizational energy. Halt execution. Begin again. |

### Scorecard Template
```
STRATEGY: [Name] | Date: [YYYY-MM-DD] | Reviewer: [Name]

Hallmark 1 (Fluff):              __ / 4
Hallmark 2 (Avoid Challenge):    __ / 4
Hallmark 3 (Goals ≠ Strategy):   __ / 4
Hallmark 4 (Bad Objectives):     __ / 4
─────────────────────────────────────
TOTAL SCORE:                     __ / 16

Verdict: [PROCEED / REVISE / DEEP REVISE / RESTART]
Key Issues: [List top 2-3 problems]
Remediation: [Specific next steps]
```

## Integration Points

### With Strategy Partner (SP)
- **SP's Quality Gate:** Analytical quality (rigor of data, internal consistency)
- **This Module's Gate:** Strategic quality (clarity of thinking, absence of bad patterns)
- **Sequence:** Run SP's analysis gate first (data quality). Then run this module (strategic quality). Both must pass.
- **Handoff:** If strategy passes both gates, move to Objective Cascade module.

### With Other Rumelt Modules
- **After Diagnostic Phase:** Use this detector to validate that challenge is named and real (Hallmark 2).
- **Before Guiding Policy:** Use Hallmark 1 to ensure policy is specific, not fluffy.
- **Before Objective Cascade:** Use Hallmark 4 to ensure objectives aren't laundry list.
- **Implementation Readiness:** If execution plans show confusion about strategy, re-run Hallmark 1 (fluff may hide in delegation).

## The Rule
**No strategy document advances without passing this detector.** Not because we're gatekeeping. Because bad strategy *costs money.* It misallocates resources, confuses teams, and burns organizational credibility when it fails. Catch it now. Fix it cheaply. Execute well.

**Rumelt's insight:** "Strategy is not goals. Strategy is clear-eyed reckoning with what's hard, plus a coordinated set of moves to win." This module ensures you have that. Nothing less.
