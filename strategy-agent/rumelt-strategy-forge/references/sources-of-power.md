# Sources of Strategic Power
> Reference: rumelt-strategy-forge | Phase: Power Assessment

Strategic power is the ability to achieve disproportionate impact with available resources. Most organizations rely on a portfolio of 2-3 sources working together. Rumelt identified eight distinct sources that create sustainable competitive advantage.

---

## 1. LEVERAGE: Pivotal Force Multiplication

**Definition:** Concentrating force at a critical point where small action produces large outcome. Not brute force—surgical precision identifying where the system is sensitive.

**Diagnostic Questions:**
- Where in our value chain does modest investment unlock exponential customer value?
- What customer friction point, if solved, creates downstream effects we can monetize?
- Are we competing on dimensions where we'll always lose, ignoring where we could dominate?
- What structural bottleneck in the industry could we eliminate with proprietary capability?
- Is our current market position amplifying or diluting our efforts?

**Real-World Example:** Netflix's leverage point was understanding that *delivery mechanism* (streaming) mattered more than content volume. Competitors built vast libraries; Netflix focused on frictionless access. That single lever (platform UX) made their entire operation more valuable.

**How to Build It:**
1. Map your value chain. Identify each customer friction point.
2. Measure sensitivity: which problems, if solved, unlock the most downstream value?
3. Audit your resources against that map. Where do you have asymmetric advantage?
4. Test: implement small, then measure multiplier effect.
5. Reinforce: channel resources into that lever; retreat from elsewhere.

**Common Mistakes:**
- Spreading investment across multiple friction points (kills leverage).
- Choosing leverage point you're weak at (technical hurdle > market hurdle).
- Mistaking difficulty for strategic importance.

**Assessment Criteria:**
- **1 (Not Present):** Competing on undifferentiated dimensions.
- **2 (Weak):** Identified pain point but unclear why it's our lever.
- **3 (Moderate):** Clear leverage point, inconsistent resource allocation.
- **4 (Strong):** All-in on one critical lever; measurable amplification.
- **5 (Dominant):** Proprietary ability to repeatedly identify and activate new levers.

## 2. PROXIMATE OBJECTIVES: Feasible Goals, Actual Resources

**Definition:** Goals close enough to reach with current resources and skill. Distant dreams sound inspiring; proximate objectives win markets.

**Diagnostic Questions:**
- Can we honestly achieve this with 18-24 months and current team?
- What specific resources/skills are we assuming we'll acquire? (Risk flag.)
- Are we setting targets based on market research or executive aspiration?
- What's the minimum viable win that moves the needle?
- Where do our resource constraints force creative problem-solving?

**Real-World Example:** Amazon in 2005 didn't set a goal "become the world's retail giant." Proximate objective: "Offer faster, cheaper delivery than competitors for books and media." Achievable. That win funded the next level.

**How to Build It:**
1. Define 3-5 year vision (distant).
2. Work backward: what must be true about this year to enable year-two success?
3. Audit: do we have or can quickly acquire the capabilities?
4. Pressure-test the goal: would we bet the business on it?
5. Document the assumption dependencies—every goal rests on unproven assumptions.

**Common Mistakes:**
- Setting goals at scale you don't have capability for yet.
- Assuming capabilities you'll "pick up" (magical thinking).
- Confusing board-friendly language with actual business possibility.

**Assessment Criteria:**
- **1 (Not Present):** Goals untethered from resources; team confused.
- **2 (Weak):** Goals seem achievable but many unproven capability assumptions.
- **3 (Moderate):** Core goal feasible; some secondary goals overreach.
- **4 (Strong):** All goals resource-aligned; clear capability dependencies tracked.
- **5 (Dominant):** Consistently set challenging but achievable targets; learn fast between iterations.

## 3. CHAIN-LINK SYSTEMS: Bottleneck Matters More Than Strength

**Definition:** System performance equals weakest link. Improving already-strong links while bottleneck remains unchanged delivers zero advantage.

**Diagnostic Questions:**
- Where's our actual system breaking? (Not where we hope it might.)
- Are we investing in improving strength or removing bottlenecks?
- What's preventing our product from delivering value faster/cheaper/better?
- If we ignore marketing and sales, what holds us back?
- Could a competitor outflank us by fixing what we're ignoring?

**Real-World Example:** A manufacturing company invested heavily in faster production equipment while their supply chain couldn't reliably deliver raw materials on time. Millions wasted. The bottleneck was logistics, not machinery.

**How to Build It:**
1. Map your critical chain: R&D → Manufacturing → Supply → Sales → Support.
2. Measure each link: throughput, latency, quality, cost.
3. Identify the one link that, if improved, enables everything else.
4. Make that link unreasonably strong (while keeping others acceptable).
5. Revisit quarterly: bottlenecks shift as you scale.

**Common Mistakes:**
- Improving what's already excellent (ego-driven).
- Not actually measuring where the system fails.
- Building redundancy in the wrong place.

**Assessment Criteria:**
- **1 (Not Present):** No understanding of system constraints.
- **2 (Weak):** Know the bottleneck, spread investments anyway.
- **3 (Moderate):** Focused investment on bottleneck; other links adequate.
- **4 (Strong):** Bottleneck link is proprietary strength; system hums.
- **5 (Dominant):** Anticipate future bottlenecks; preemptively strengthen them.

## 4. DESIGN: Integrated Systems Outperform Modular Ones

**Definition:** Tight coupling of strategy, resources, policies, and execution. Power emerges from mutual adjustment of parts, not sum of parts.

**Diagnostic Questions:**
- If we described our strategy, would operations, sales, and product all tell the same story?
- Are our incentive structures aligned with our stated strategy?
- Do we have capability silos that prevent cross-functional efficiency?
- Could a competitor copy our individual elements but not the system?
- What takes talent and time to copy: the pieces or how they fit?

**Real-World Example:** Costco's power isn't bulk distribution (copyable) or low prices (easy to match). It's the integrated design: employee pay → low turnover → customer service experience → membership loyalty → inventory turns → profit margin. Change one element, the system breaks.

**How to Build It:**
1. Define core strategy in one sentence.
2. Map required capabilities, investments, culture, incentives for that strategy.
3. Audit misalignment: where do policies contradict strategy?
4. Realign: change incentives, training, hiring criteria, resource allocation.
5. Make it difficult to partially copy—that's where power lives.

**Common Mistakes:**
- Bolting strategy onto existing structure without redesign.
- Underinvesting in cultural alignment (the glue).
- Treating design as one-time; it requires constant maintenance.

**Assessment Criteria:**
- **1 (Not Present):** Strategy exists on paper; operations don't reflect it.
- **2 (Weak):** Strategy clear; 50% of systems aligned, 50% contradictory.
- **3 (Moderate):** Major systems aligned; some silos remain.
- **4 (Strong):** Integrated across functions; difficult to partially copy.
- **5 (Dominant):** Self-reinforcing system; competitive moat deepens as you scale.

## 5. FOCUS: Concentrating Effort on High-Impact Subset

**Definition:** Full weight of capabilities on narrow set of customer problems or markets. Defocused = attempt everything, accomplish nothing decisive.

**Diagnostic Questions:**
- Can we articulate the 2-3 customer problems we're solving better than anyone?
- What are we explicitly *not* building/serving? (Omission reveals focus.)
- Does every product decision ladder back to core focus?
- Are we saying yes to adjacent opportunities we should refuse?
- What would happen if we killed our lowest-revenue product lines?

**Real-World Example:** Slack focused obsessively on enterprise team communication for tech-forward companies. They said no to consumers, no to regulated industries initially, no to public channels-first design. That focus enabled product excellence competitors couldn't match.

**How to Build It:**
1. Define your target customer segment with brutal specificity.
2. Identify their top 3-5 problems.
3. Commit: we will solve these better than anyone; we will not solve everything.
4. Build product, sales, marketing in alignment with that constraint.
5. Kill or spin off anything that dilutes focus.

**Common Mistakes:**
- Fear-based scope expansion ("but what if they need...?").
- Confusing focus with smallness—focused ≠ limited, just directional.
- Losing focus as company grows without deliberate recommitment.

**Assessment Criteria:**
- **1 (Not Present):** Attempting to serve many customer segments equally.
- **2 (Weak):** Stated focus; product roadmap contradicts it.
- **3 (Moderate):** Clear focus; some dilution from adjacent bets.
- **4 (Strong):** All efforts ladder to core focus; disciplined prioritization.
- **5 (Dominant):** Focus so clear it shapes culture; makes hard tradeoffs easy.

## 6. GROWTH THROUGH ADVANTAGE: Build From Strength

**Definition:** Healthy growth flows from existing competitive advantage. Growing without advantage = fattening not strengthening. You're just moving cash around.

**Diagnostic Questions:**
- Why do customers choose us? (Real reason, not marketing language.)
- Does our growth engine leverage that advantage or create new dependencies?
- Are we growing into markets we don't have advantage in?
- Does revenue growth correlate with margin improvement or just volume?
- What would happen if a well-funded competitor entered our core market?

**Real-World Example:** Microsoft's move into cloud (Azure) leveraged existing enterprise relationships and security reputation. Advantage + growth multiplication. Compare to their smartphone efforts—no advantage, just cash burn.

**How to Build It:**
1. Define your current competitive advantage with precision.
2. Map adjacent markets where that advantage transfers or amplifies.
3. Tier bets: extend advantage (high %), explore new (low % portfolio reserve).
4. Measure: does revenue growth improve your competitive position or just dilute it?
5. Course-correct: kill growth initiatives that erode advantage.

**Common Mistakes:**
- Pursuing growth in markets without advantage (vanity metrics).
- Assuming advantage transfers; don't extrapolate without testing.
- Investing equally in core vs. exploratory—should be 70/30 minimum.

**Assessment Criteria:**
- **1 (Not Present):** Growth scattered; unclear what drives customer choice.
- **2 (Weak):** Core advantage identified; growth strategy dilutes it.
- **3 (Moderate):** 60% of growth extends advantage; 40% is exploratory.
- **4 (Strong):** Growth concentrated where advantage is strongest; expanding moat.
- **5 (Dominant):** Each growth move amplifies core advantage; compound effect visible.

## 7. USING DYNAMICS: Riding Waves of Change

**Definition:** Anticipate and position for structural shifts (technology, regulation, demographics). Early positioning creates asymmetric advantage before competitors recognize the wave.

**Diagnostic Questions:**
- What technology is maturing in adjacent markets that could reshape ours?
- Are regulatory shifts opening or closing our competitive space?
- How is customer behavior changing, and are we ahead or behind the trend?
- What capabilities do we have that become more valuable in next-gen market?
- Where are we overinvested in old structures we need to shed?

**Real-World Example:** Apple recognized mobile would reshape computing *before* the smartphone was mainstream. They built OS-level advantage before competitors realized what was happening. By the time rivals responded, iOS was entrenched.

**How to Build It:**
1. Scan: what's changing in your industry horizon (18-36 months)?
2. Assess: which shifts amplify our strengths? Which expose weaknesses?
3. Position: make small early bets on waves that amplify strength.
4. Shed: systematically reduce reliance on old structures.
5. Communicate: help team understand *why* you're making these bets.

**Common Mistakes:**
- Mistaking trend noise for structural shift.
- Overinvesting in early trends; losing core business in process.
- Moving too slowly once wave is clear (by then it's too late).

**Assessment Criteria:**
- **1 (Not Present):** Reactive; surprised by industry shifts.
- **2 (Weak):** See changes happening; unclear what to do.
- **3 (Moderate):** Placed some bets on emerging waves; core business protected.
- **4 (Strong):** Consistently positioned ahead of shifts; capabilities align with future.
- **5 (Dominant):** Shape the narrative of change; position others as followers.

---

## 8. INERTIA & ENTROPY: Your Advantage, Your Vulnerability

**Definition:** Competitor inertia (resistance to change) = your advantage. Your own inertia = vulnerability. Organizations naturally drift toward entropy unless actively resisting.

**Diagnostic Questions:**
- Where are our competitors stuck defending old business models?
- How are we protecting yesterday's competitive advantage at tomorrow's expense?
- What beliefs do we hold that limit our thinking?
- Are we investing in renewal, or just optimizing existing business?
- What would we do differently if starting fresh today?

**Real-World Example:** Kodak invented digital photography but couldn't cannibalize film revenue. Their inertia (protect the cash cow) was Fujifilm's advantage. By the time Kodak moved, Fujifilm had already rebuilt around digital and sensors.

**How to Build It:**
1. Audit: where are competitors locked in by old structures?
2. Exploit: design strategy that targets their inertia.
3. Self-check: what inertia are *we* tolerating? Name it explicitly.
4. Invest: allocate budget to cannibalize own products before competitors do.
5. Culture: reward questioning, experimentation, calculated failure.

**Common Mistakes:**
- Acknowledging competitor inertia but not building strategy around it.
- Underestimating your own inertia; assuming management is smarter than it is.
- Failing to invest in renewal because current business is profitable.

**Assessment Criteria:**
- **1 (Not Present):** No awareness of inertia; complacency.
- **2 (Weak):** See competitor constraints; not exploiting them strategically.
- **3 (Moderate):** Actively exploiting competitor inertia; some internal renewal.
- **4 (Strong):** Designed to overcome own inertia; constant managed renewal.
- **5 (Dominant):** Inertia used as strategic weapon; organization structured for change.

## Power Portfolio Framework: Combinations Create Compound Advantage

Rumelt's insight: most durable strategies combine 2-3 sources. The combination creates interdependency that's harder to copy than any single source.

**High-Synergy Combinations:**

| Combination | Why It Works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Leverage + Design | Singular focus (leverage) locked in by integrated systems | Costco's discount + membership design |
| Focus + Chain-Link | Narrow market lets you obsess over system strength | Tesla's vertical integration for EVs |
| Growth Through Advantage + Dynamics | Ride waves with existing strength; don't chase trends generically | AWS expanding on cloud infrastructure lead |
| Proximate Objectives + Inertia | Competitors can't move fast enough to block your next level | Netflix moving from DVD → streaming → content |
| Design + Inertia | System so integrated that competitor inertia looks worse by contrast | Apple ecosystem vs. Android fragmentation |

**Weak/No-Synergy Combinations:**
- Leverage + Growth (growing without advantage): dilutes leverage.
- Focus + Dynamics (chasing trend outside your sweet spot): kills focus.
- Multiple leverage points: leverage loses power through splitting effort.

## Power Assessment Scoring

For each source, score 1-5:

**1 = Not Present:** Element absent or invisible in strategy/operations.
**2 = Weak:** Element identified but inconsistently applied; contradicted elsewhere.
**3 = Moderate:** Element functional and contributing; room for reinforcement.
**4 = Strong:** Element is material to competitive position; difficult to copy.
**5 = Dominant:** Element is durable moat; shapes market dynamics.

**Interpretation:**
- **Portfolio average 3.5+:** Solid strategy. Sustainable advantage.
- **Portfolio average 2.5-3.4:** Fragmented. At risk from focused competitor.
- **Portfolio average <2.5:** Vulnerable. No clear source of power.

## Industry Power Patterns

**Tech Startups** → Typically leverage + focus + dynamics
- Small team must identify one powerful lever (product-market fit).
- Navigate fast-moving landscape by anticipating waves.
- Danger: lack of design = hard to scale without culture decay.

**Mature Industrials** → Chain-link + design + inertia (exploiting others')
- Operational excellence through system optimization.
- Integrated supply chains, manufacturing, logistics.
- Danger: their own inertia. Miss disruptive shifts.

**Enterprise Software** → Design + chain-link + proximate objectives
- Integration matters more than features (vendor lock-in).
- Complex sales and implementation become moat.
- Danger: new entrants with simpler design; market shifts to cloud-native.

**Retail/E-Commerce** → Growth through advantage + leverage + dynamics
- Existing customer base → expansion lever.
- Capital and logistics advantage compounds.
- Danger: Amazon enters category and leverage shifts against you.

**Professional Services** → Focus + proximate objectives + design
- Narrow expertise + integrated firm culture.
- Next engagement builds on previous relationship.
- Danger: talent-dependent; inertia kills firms when markets shift.

## Power Gap Analysis: Your Advantage vs. Competitor Asymmetry

This is Rumelt's core insight: strategy isn't about being strong everywhere. It's about asymmetry.

**Gap Analysis Process:**

1. **Score yourself and top 3 competitors across all 8 sources** (use 1-5 scale above).
2. **Identify gaps:** Where do you score significantly higher (3+ point difference)?
3. **Validate gaps:** Is this real or self-deception? Get external perspective.
4. **Determine if gaps are sustainable** (can't be copied) or temporary (lucky timing).
5. **Check competitor blind spots:** Where might they be vulnerable to your strength?

**Example Framework:**

```
Source               | You | Competitor A | Competitor B | Gap Insight
Leverage             | 4   | 2            | 3            | Your leverage in X is defensible
Chain-Link Systems   | 3   | 4            | 3            | Competitor A's supply excellence—learn it
Design Integration   | 3   | 2            | 4            | Competitor B's culture stronger; you're fragmented
Focus                | 5   | 3            | 2            | Your focus is your biggest advantage
Growth Through Adv.  | 2   | 3            | 4            | Both competitors growing smarter than you
Dynamics             | 3   | 2            | 4            | Competitor B anticipating shifts better
```

**Strategic Implication:** You have a window of opportunity in Focus (5 vs. 2-3). Competitors with Design/Growth advantage will eventually catch up. Your move: compound the Focus advantage into Design before they do.
