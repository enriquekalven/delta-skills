# The Strategy Kernel
> Reference: rumelt-strategy-forge | Phase: Kernel Construction

The strategy kernel is your irreducible core. Without all three elements—**Diagnosis**, **Guiding Policy**, and **Coherent Actions**—you have fragments, not strategy. This reference teaches you to build each with precision and integrate them for power.

## Part 1: Constructing Diagnosis

**Diagnosis** reduces complex reality to a named, explainable challenge. It's your story—not a symptom list or a goal. A good diagnosis tells you *why* action is needed and *which* actions matter.

### The Three Moves of Diagnosis

**1. Name the Challenge, Not the Problem**
- Problem: "Revenue is declining." Challenge: "Our core customer is shifting to competitors who bundle services we don't offer—we've optimized for yesterday's market."
- Problem: "Employee retention is 60%." Challenge: "We attract talent for prestige but deliver a culture of overwork without mentorship or growth paths—selection is wrong."
- The challenge connects *what's happening* to *why it matters*. It narrows focus.

**2. Build Explanatory Logic**
- Don't list obstacles. Show how they connect. "The market has shifted to sustainability criteria. Our supply chain was built for cost, not footprint. Our brand, built on value-pricing, can't carry a premium product. Competitors who repositioned early now own customer loyalty."
- This logic makes clear what you must change and what you can ignore.

**3. Face Reality Unflinchingly**
- A diagnosis that doesn't hurt is probably wrong. It must acknowledge what you lack, not just what's wrong with the world.
- Acceptable diagnosis: "We're a strong brand in declining channels." Weak diagnosis: "Channels are declining." The first forces action; the second invites passivity.

### Quality Checks for Diagnosis

- **Leads to different strategies:** If three competing diagnoses lead to the same actions, your diagnosis isn't precise enough.
- **Explains more than one symptom:** A diagnosis should coherently explain revenue decline, employee exits, and competitive losses together—not separately.
- **Is testable:** You should be able to gather data that confirms or refutes it. "Our team lacks creativity" fails. "We hire for reliability over adaptability, and our hiring metrics correlate with slower innovation cycles" passes.
- **Creates a frame, not a ceiling:** "Our advantage is in B2B relationships" opens options (deepen relationships, monetize differently, extend to adjacent segments). "We are a B2B company" closes them.

## Part 2: Constructing Guiding Policy

**Guiding Policy** is your approach—the constraints and priorities that direct action without dictating specifics. Think of it as strategic guardrails.

### The Logic of Guiding Policy

A guiding policy says "we will emphasize X and de-emphasize Y." It says what you're *not* doing as clearly as what you are. It flows directly from diagnosis.

- **Diagnosis:** We're strong in relationships but locked in a mature, commoditizing market.
- **Policy:** We will move up-market to complex, relationship-intensive deals where our strength compounds. We will not pursue volume or market-share gains.
- **What this rules out:** Automation, standardization, pricing competition, new geographic expansion (unless it's where complex deals cluster).

### Quality Checks for Guiding Policy

- **Creates advantage via strength:** Does it amplify something you're genuinely good at? Weak: "We will be faster." Strong: "We will compete on decision speed by centralizing authority and reducing approval layers—we're already lean."
- **Is specific about trade-offs:** "We will prioritize quality" is not a policy. "We will prioritize product life-cycle cost to end-users over initial price point, accepting 15–20% higher entry cost" is.
- **Would surprise a competitor:** If your main rival wouldn't be bothered by your policy, it's not a meaningful constraint.
- **Is durable but not permanent:** A guiding policy lasts 3–7 years typically, until the underlying diagnosis shifts. It's not a company value; it's a strategic choice.

### Anti-Pattern Examples and Fixes

| Bad Policy | Why It Fails | Better Version |
|---|---|---|
| "We will innovate aggressively" | Tells no one what to actually do; costs explode | "We will invest 12% of revenue in adjacent-market innovation, defined as products we can distribute through existing channels with <2 years time-to-market" |
| "Customer obsession is core" | Every company says this; doesn't constrain action | "We will price for customer switching cost, not feature value, meaning we accept slower feature velocity than competitors" |
| "We will be the technology leader" | Undefined, expensive, often wrong | "We will own the proprietary backend that powers our differentiation; we will integrate third-party frontends, accepting dependency" |
| "Scale efficiently" | Conflicts with every other goal; invisible trade-offs | "We will limit customer count to 200, with >$5M ACV, to preserve high-touch service margins and retention" |
| "Transform the industry" | Massive cost, low probability | "We will build the first secure, interoperable platform for [specific workflow], targeting the top 10% of practitioners" |

## Part 3: Constructing Coherent Actions

**Coherent Actions** are specific, time-bound moves that reinforce each other and carry out the policy. Individually, each action supports the diagnosis and policy. Together, they create a system where removing any piece weakens the whole.

### The Anatomy of Coherent Actions

Each action must be:
- **Specific enough to fund, schedule, and measure.** Not "improve hiring" but "build a technical recruiter team, target 6 hires in 90 days, source from X networks, track time-to-productivity."
- **Sequenced.** Some actions unlock others. You can't scale channels before you have channel-grade operations.
- **Resource-constrained but sufficient.** You must be able to do them with the budget and talent you have or can realistically access.

### The Coherence Multiplier

Coherence is not additive; it's multiplicative. Three unrelated actions of average quality = low impact. Three tightly interlocking actions = exponential power.

Example coherence multiplier:
- **Action 1:** Move sales to a six-person account team focused on $10M+ deals.
- **Action 2:** Build a "implementation excellence" function to ensure customers succeed in their first 90 days.
- **Action 3:** Price based on customer outcome (revenue impact), not feature count or seat count.

These three actions reinforce:
- Sales action + Implementation action: Bigger deals require flawless execution; implementation ensures it happens.
- Implementation action + Pricing action: Outcome-based pricing makes implementation data visible; implementation team has strong incentive to prove value.
- Pricing action + Sales action: Outcome pricing attracts deal-hungry salespeople; removes commoditizing price wars.

Removing any one degrades the others. This is a kernel, not a list.

### Quality Checks for Actions

- **Do they reinforce each other?** Adjust them iteratively. If one action succeeds and the others stall, they're not coherent.
- **Are they feasible?** The team must be able to execute them. "Build a new product line" is action-like; "ship a new product line in Q3 with the current team" forces clarity.
- **Can you remove one without weakening the system?** If yes, it's not coherent; it's decorative.

### Anti-Pattern Examples and Fixes

| Bad Action Set | Why It Fails | Better Version |
|---|---|---|
| Build product → Hire salespeople → Launch marketing campaign (simultaneous) | Nothing unlocks anything; all three compete for attention; failures cascade | Build product (Q1) → Pilot with 10 customers (Q2) → Hire sales+marketing for proven customer segment (Q3). Each phase validates the next. |
| "Improve customer retention" + "Reduce support costs" + "Move to self-serve" | Retention and cost are in tension; self-serve without support training fails | "Build self-serve onboarding (30-min ramp)" + "Keep human support for complex issues" + "Measure cohort retention, adjust onboarding based on data" (retention informs support targets). |
| Launch in Europe + Build partnerships + Enter mid-market (simultaneously) | Resources scattered; mid-market and partnerships often compete for the same team | Focus: Enter mid-market (Q1–Q3) with partnerships in your existing geography. Once model is proven, apply it to Europe (Q4+). |
| Reduce headcount + Increase feature velocity + Raise prices | Contradictory; cost-cutting erodes velocity; price increases alienate the shrinking customer base | Context-dependent. If diagnosis is "we're commoditized," then reduce headcount in delivery (outsource proven work), increase velocity in proprietary differentiation only, raise prices for new positioning. |
| "Diversify revenue" + "Stay focused on core" | Incoherent; diversification without focus is expensive; focus without diversification is fragile | "Expand into adjacent applications of our core technology where we can leverage existing R&D" (adds revenue without new cost structure). |

## Part 4: Worked Example

**Scenario:** TechServe, a $40M SaaS company, provides workflow automation to mid-market finance teams. Growth has stalled (3% YoY). Competitors offer richer features; larger platforms are moving down-market. The team feels unfocused and burned out.

### Diagnosis
*"We're caught between markets. We're too expensive for small buyers but lack the depth for enterprise buyers. Our product roadmap reflects customer requests, not strategic positioning—we've become a feature factory. Our cost structure requires $100K+ ACV to be profitable, but our brand attracts $50K-80K deals, creating a margin death spiral."*

This diagnosis:
- Explains stalled growth (can't win small, can't win large)
- Explains team burnout (no clear priority; feature requests are infinite)
- Explains margin pressure (product costs rising, price constrained)
- Leads to specific action, not vague fixes

### Guiding Policy
*"We will position as the workflow automation platform for the top 5% of finance organizations by complexity—those with $500M+ revenue and sophisticated multi-subsidiary treasury operations. We will prioritize depth of customization and integration flexibility over ease-of-use. We will not compete on price or breadth of features."*

This policy:
- Flows from diagnosis (focuses on markets where $100K+ ACV is natural)
- Rules things out (no self-serve, no free tier, no feature parity races)
- Creates advantage (our small size is now an asset—we can customize; large platforms can't)
- Would surprise competitors (they'll assume we're chasing market share)

### Coherent Actions
1. **Sales:** Move from transactional sales to relationship-driven, 12-month deal cycles. Hire two enterprise account executives (known in your network) and eliminate inside sales. Target 4–6 new deals in 18 months.

2. **Product:** Freeze the public roadmap. Allocate 60% of engineering to custom integrations for each customer, 40% to shared platform depth. Accept that this kills feature parity with competitors—it's the point.

3. **Pricing:** Move to outcome-based contracts: $200K upfront + $X per unit of process digitized. Eliminates price shopping; aligns us with customer success.

4. **Operations:** Build a "customer success engineering" team (2 people initially) dedicated to implementation and deep customization for each account. Make this a profit center.

5. **Marketing:** Stop broad awareness campaigns. Sponsor executive forums and publish thought leadership on treasury optimization for large organizations. Goal: be the known expert in a narrow space.

Why these cohere:
- Sales targets (relationship-driven) + Product (custom work) + Operations (success engineering): Each deal requires handholding; we're equipped for it; competitors aren't.
- Pricing (outcome-based) + Product (customization) + Operations (success engineering): We can charge for proven value; customization lets us deliver it; operations ensures we do.
- Removing any one piece breaks the system: Without custom engineering, outcome pricing is a risk. Without success ops, custom work fails. Without relationship sales, we can't build the trust to support deep contracts.

## Part 5: Integration with Strategy Partner

**Strategy Partner (SP)** conducts industry and competitive analysis. This analysis feeds the kernel:

- **SP's industry analysis** (market segmentation, buyer power, supplier power, substitutes, competitive intensity) **→ informs Diagnosis.** Good diagnosis sits in an industry context. SP's work reveals where your competitive position is actually strong.

- **SP's competitive positioning** (how you compare on cost, features, speed, relationships, etc.) **→ informs Guiding Policy.** Policy must amplify genuine advantages identified by SP's analysis.

- **SP's capability assessment** (what you can realistically build or hire) **→ constrains Coherent Actions.** Actions that require capabilities you don't have and can't build in the timeline are fantasy, not strategy.

The kernel and SP analysis are a closed loop. Diagnosis without SP's context is guessing. SP's analysis without a kernel to guide it is interesting but inert.

## Part 6: Output Template

Use this template to build and communicate your kernel:

```markdown
## KERNEL: [One-sentence strategic goal]

### DIAGNOSIS
[2–3 sentences explaining the challenge, not symptoms]
- Why this matters: [What happens if unchanged]
- Key insight: [The insight that changes how you see the problem]

### GUIDING POLICY
[1–2 sentences on overall approach]
- What we emphasize: [Specific strength we're amplifying]
- What we don't do: [Explicit trade-offs/ruled-out options]
- Timeline: [3–5 years typically]

### COHERENT ACTIONS
[5–7 specific, time-bound moves]
1. [Action] | Responsibility: [Owner] | Timeline: [When] | Success metric: [How you know it worked]
2. [Action] | Responsibility: [Owner] | Timeline: [When] | Success metric: [How you know it worked]
...

### COHERENCE PROOF
[Show how removing any one action weakens others. 2–3 sentences.]

### ASSUMPTIONS
[What must be true for this kernel to work?]
- Assumption 1: [Testable]
- Assumption 2: [Testable]
```

## The Test

You have a good kernel if:
1. **Each element is specific enough to argue about.** Vague kernels produce vague execution.
2. **Different diagnoses would produce different strategies.** If you'd do the same thing no matter the diagnosis, you didn't diagnose.
3. **A skilled competitor reading your guiding policy would adjust their strategy.** If they'd ignore it, it's not meaningful.
4. **You can explain why removing any action weakens the others.** If actions are independent, you have a list, not a kernel.
5. **The entire team can explain it in 90 seconds.** If it requires a presentation, it's too complex.

A 10/10 kernel is precise, coherent, and constraining. It enables fast decisions because choices clearly align or misalign with it.
