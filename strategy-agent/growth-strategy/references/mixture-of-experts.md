# Mixture of Experts: Growth Strategy Review Panel

This reference provides a 5-expert panel for reviewing and stress-testing growth strategies before execution. Each expert brings a unique lens and will catch blind spots the others miss.

---

## The Five Expert Personas

### Expert 1: Growth Equity Investor

**Background:** 15+ years investing in growth-stage companies; seen 100+ growth strategies succeed and fail; obsessed with pattern recognition and unit economics.

**Worldview:** "Growth is capital allocation. Show me the CAC, LTV, and payback period, or you haven't thought about growth."

**What They Notice:**
- Unit economics are crystal clear or fuzzy (red flag if fuzzy)
- Capital efficiency: Is this growth capital-efficient?
- Scalability: Does this model scale or just grow by adding more capital?
- Margin compression: Will growth initiatives destroy profitability?
- Comparables: How does this compare to similar companies' growth?

**Key Questions to Answer:**
1. What is the blended CAC and LTV per customer segment? Is it 1:3+ across all meaningful segments?
2. At what scale does this growth lever become unprofitable? (Identify the breaking point)
3. What is the revenue per dollar of capital invested? (If <$2, be suspicious)
4. How much does the gross margin change with scale? (If compressed, why?)
5. What's the payback period per growth initiative? (If >18 months, why continue?)
6. How capital-intensive is each growth lever vs. competitors? (Are you overspending?)
7. What are the unit economics compared to industry benchmarks? (Are you competitive?)

**Scoring Criteria:**
- Unit economics crystal clear: +2
- Blended CAC:LTV ≥1:3: +2
- Payback period <12 months: +1
- Gross margin stable or improving with scale: +1
- Capital efficiency competitive vs. benchmarks: +1
- Path to profitability clear: +1
- Revenue per capital invested >2:1: +1
- **Max score: 9**

**Common Critiques:**
- "Unit economics are too fuzzy. You're hoping, not calculating."
- "CAC is declining with scale, but LTV is declining faster. This doesn't survive 10x."
- "You're assuming 80% gross margin, but at enterprise scale, it's 70%. Re-run the model."
- "Payback period is 18 months, but you only have 12 months of runway. This kills the business."
- "Capital efficiency is 1.2:1. Competitors do 3:1. Why should capital fund your strategy?"

### Expert 2: Seasoned Operator

**Background:** Has scaled 3 companies from $10M to $500M+; has made hiring decisions, built go-to-market teams, managed through downturns; pragmatist about execution.

**Worldview:** "A good plan executed is better than a perfect plan executed badly. Show me the team, the process, and the metrics that matter."

**What They Notice:**
- Do you have the right leader for this growth lever? (Execution matters as much as strategy)
- Is the team capacity realistic? (Can they really do this while running core business?)
- Are metrics clear and leading? (Do you know if you're winning weekly, or just quarterly?)
- Is the roadmap sequenced correctly? (Prerequisites before dependents?)
- Is the culture/org design set up for this growth? (Or does growth require new org structure?)

**Key Questions to Answer:**
1. Who owns this growth initiative? Do you have the right leader? (Track record in this type of growth)
2. What's the full-time equivalent cost of this initiative? (Not just new hires; include reallocation)
3. What are the 3-5 leading indicators you'll track weekly? (Not lagging indicators like revenue)
4. What are the top 3 execution risks? (What could kill this?)
5. How will you know in month 3 if this is working? (Clear gates, not vague metrics)
6. What org changes are required to make this work? (New roles, new reporting lines, new culture?)
7. How do you avoid this growth initiative distracting the core business? (Protection mechanisms)

**Scoring Criteria:**
- Owner has proven track record in this type of growth: +2
- Team capacity is realistic (not stretched beyond limits): +2
- Leading metrics are clear and trackable weekly: +1
- 30-60-90 day plan is specific and sequenced: +1
- Execution risks identified and mitigated: +1
- Org structure supports initiative (no competing incentives): +1
- Core business has protection mechanisms (not distracted): +1
- **Max score: 9**

**Common Critiques:**
- "You're asking the enterprise sales lead to launch a self-serve channel. That's a different skillset. She'll fail."
- "Your leading metric is 'number of partnerships signed.' That's lagging. You won't know if it's working until month 6."
- "You haven't accounted for the engineering work to support this growth. Product team is already 110% allocated."
- "Three years ago, a similar company tried this and failed. What's different about your execution?"
- "Your 30-60-90 plan has 12 action items per month. That's not a plan; it's a wish list. You'll hit 4 of 12."

### Expert 3: Market Entry Specialist

**Background:** 15+ years doing geographic expansion, market entry, and adjacency moves; knows every failure mode; understands competitive response and localization friction.

**Worldview:** "Market entry is deceptively hard. Most failures are because someone underestimated how different the new market is from the home market."

**What They Notice:**
- Is the new market actually different from home market? (Or are you assuming similarity you don't have?)
- What's the competitive landscape? (Are incumbents sleeping or will they wake up?)
- How are you localizing? (Language translation is easy; customer preference differences are hard)
- What's your entry mode and is it right for this market? (Direct vs. partnership vs. acquisition decision tree)
- Do you have local expertise? (Hiring the wrong country manager is expensive)
- What's your beachhead? (Trying to win too much of the market too fast is common death)

**Key Questions to Answer:**
1. Name the #1 difference between home market and new market. (How is customer buying behavior different?)
2. Who are the 3-5 incumbent competitors? What will their response be? (They'll notice you; assume response in 3-6mo)
3. What localization is required beyond language? (Payment methods, compliance, regulatory, pricing?)
4. Why this entry mode (direct sales, partnership, acquisition, JV)? (Clear decision logic required)
5. Who is your local leader/partner? Do they have credibility in this market? (Reference-check required)
6. What's your beachhead within this market? (Trying to win entire market = likely to lose)
7. What's your competitive advantage in new market? (Why will you win when you're entering as unknown?)

**Scoring Criteria:**
- New market differences clearly articulated: +2
- Competitive landscape mapped (3+ competitors identified): +2
- Entry mode justified with clear decision logic: +1
- Localization requirements beyond language identified: +1
- Local leader/partner has credible track record: +1
- Beachhead is narrow and defensible: +1
- Competitive advantage in new market is clear: +1
- **Max score: 9**

**Common Critiques:**
- "You're entering Germany assuming it's like US. You don't speak German, haven't talked to 10 customers there, and you're guessing."
- "Incumbent competitor is Salesforce. They own 80% of enterprise market in Germany. Your unit economics need to be 2x better to displace them."
- "You're hiring a local sales leader from your home office instead of recruiting someone in-country with relationships. You'll lose 6 months to culture shock."
- "Your beachhead is 'all SaaS companies in Germany.' That's not a beachhead; that's the whole market. Your beachhead should be 'Series B SaaS raised <$10M.'"
- "You're assuming a partnership with local distributor will solve go-to-market. Last company tried that and the distributor deprioritized them."

### Expert 4: Platform Strategist

**Background:** Built ecosystem businesses; understands network effects, two-sided platforms, developer economics; thinks in graphs and flywheels.

**Worldview:** "Network effects are the only sustainable 10x growth. Everything else is execution excellence. Design your business to compound or don't design it at all."

**What They Notice:**
- Does the growth strategy create network effects? (Direct, indirect, data, marketplace?)
- Is the flywheel tight or loose? (Cycle time and viral coefficient matter)
- Are you thinking about complementors and supply side? (Not just demand side)
- What's the critical mass to bootstrap the flywheel? (How many users before it's valuable?)
- Is the platform architecture defensible? (Can competitors clone this?)
- What's the developer/partner experience? (Can they build on your platform easily?)
- Are there data moats? (Does proprietary data create advantage?)

**Key Questions to Answer:**
1. Does this growth strategy exploit network effects? (If no, why not?)
2. What is the viral coefficient and loop tightness? (Is it compounding or decaying?)
3. What's the critical mass where the product becomes valuable? (When does flywheel ignite?)
4. Who are the complementors/supply side? (Can they build on your platform?)
5. What is the developer/partner experience? (Easy 1-click integration or requires custom work?)
6. What data moat exists? (Does proprietary data create advantage no competitor has?)
7. Can competitors easily copy this network effect? (Is it defensible?)

**Scoring Criteria:**
- Network effects are core to growth strategy (not afterthought): +2
- Viral coefficient > 1 or clear path to >1: +2
- Viral loop is tight (<30 days cycle time): +1
- Critical mass to bootstrap is achievable: +1
- Complementor/supply-side economics are positive: +1
- Developer experience is excellent (not just adequate): +1
- Data moat or other defensibility exists: +1
- **Max score: 9**

**Common Critiques:**
- "This growth strategy is all about direct sales and paid marketing. Neither compounds. You're spending to grow, not building a moat."
- "Your viral coefficient is 0.7. That's decaying. You'll need to pour money in every month to maintain growth."
- "You've designed for single-sided product, but complementors could add 10x value. Why aren't you thinking about the ecosystem?"
- "Your critical mass is 100K users. You'll spend $10M to get there. Competitors will do it in parallel for cheaper."
- "Developer experience is bad (complex API, slow docs, no sandbox). Few developers will build on your platform."

### Expert 5: Red Team Adversary

**Background:** Systematically finds how good plans fail; experienced at competitive response modeling; devil's advocate by design; looks for assumptions that could kill the strategy.

**Worldview:** "Every strategy will be attacked. Assume the worst and build for it. Most plans fail because someone didn't ask 'What if I'm wrong?'"

**What They Notice:**
- What assumptions are baked in? (Which, if wrong, kills the entire strategy?)
- How will competitors respond? (Assume they're not sleeping)
- What's the regulatory/legal risk? (Have you thought this through?)
- What could break operationally? (Where does the plan unravel?)
- What's the downside scenario? (Best case you know the strategy; downside is the real test)
- Is the team overconfident? (Red flags: "competitors won't notice," "we'll move faster," "market is ready")

**Key Questions to Answer:**
1. What is the single most dangerous assumption in this plan? (If wrong, whole strategy fails)
2. How will the largest competitor respond in the first 6 months? (Game-theory competitive response)
3. What regulatory or legal risks exist? (Have you consulted external experts?)
4. What is the downside scenario? (What happens if growth is 50% of forecast?)
5. What could break operationally? (Team burnout, product issues, partner failure?)
6. What's the exit plan if this doesn't work? (Can you pivot or recover?)
7. What signals would tell you to kill this early? (Clear kill criteria, or gradual death?)

**Scoring Criteria:**
- Core assumptions are challenged and stress-tested: +2
- Competitive response is modeled (not assumed away): +2
- Regulatory/legal risks are identified and mitigated: +1
- Downside scenario is articulated (not dismissed): +1
- Operational risks and mitigation plans exist: +1
- Team is appropriately humble (not overconfident): +1
- Exit plan or pivot option is clear: +1
- **Max score: 9**

**Common Critiques:**
- "Your core assumption is 'SMB market wants our product.' You have 5 customer conversations. That's not validation."
- "Competitor Salesforce will respond to market development by bundling this feature. They'll own this in 12 months."
- "You're assuming GDPR compliance will be straightforward. It's not. Budget 6+ months and $500K for legal."
- "Downside scenario: growth is 50% of forecast. You've planned for 10 people; hiring freezes at 5. Now you're underwater."
- "You're planning to hire 30 people in 6 months. That's assuming hiring works perfectly and people ramp fast. What if either fails?"

## Expert Panel Review Process

### Step 1: Provide Growth Plan to Panel

Each expert reviews the growth strategy independently. They score based on their domain criteria (see above).

```
REVIEW TEMPLATE (Per Expert)

Expert: [Name]
Domain: [Growth Equity Investor / Operator / Market Entry / Platform / Red Team]
Score: [0-9]

Strengths (2-3):
  - [What the strategy does well from my lens]

Concerns (2-3):
  - [What could go wrong from my lens]

Questions (2-3):
  - [What needs clarification or rethinking]

Recommendation:
  [ ] Pass — Strong strategy, proceed
  [ ] Conditional Pass — Good strategy, but address concerns first
  [ ] Reject — Too many risks; needs rework
```

Aggregate scores and identify common themes.

## Worked Example: Panel Review of Market Entry Strategy

```
STRATEGY: Enter Japan SaaS market with direct sales (enterprise focus)

EXPERT 1: GROWTH EQUITY INVESTOR
  Concern: "Unit economics in Japan are worse than US"
    - CAC $20K (Japan has fewer warm intros than US; longer search)
    - LTV $60K (same ACV as US, but customer acquisition cost higher)
    - Ratio: 1:3 (vs. US 1:4)
    - Payback: 18 months (vs. US 12 months)
    Score: 6/9 (borderline acceptable but not ideal)

EXPERT 2: SEASONED OPERATOR
  Concern: "You're hiring wrong person as Japan Country Manager"
    - Candidate is from HQ; has never lived in Japan
    - Will need 6+ months to understand market, build network
    - Lost time + wrong hires = delay and burnout
    - Recommendation: Hire in-country, proven Japan track record
    Score: 5/9 (high execution risk)

EXPERT 3: MARKET ENTRY SPECIALIST
  Concern: "Competitor Salesforce already dominates Japan market"
    - Salesforce has 70% market share in Japan
    - Relationships with major resellers are locked in
    - You entering as unknown = uphill battle
    - Recommendation: Find differentiation or partner with competitor against Salesforce
    Score: 4/9 (very competitive, hard to win)

EXPERT 4: PLATFORM STRATEGIST
  Comment: "This strategy has no network effects thinking"
    - Direct sales is capital-intensive, doesn't compound
    - No partnership/ecosystem approach
    - Recommendation: Partner with local SI instead of direct sales
    Score: 6/9 (valid strategy but not leveraging leverage)

EXPERT 5: RED TEAM ADVERSARY
  Concern: "You have zero Japan expertise and assumed you could hire it quickly"
    - Hiring takes 6 months
    - Wrong hire = 18 month loss
    - Assumption: "Japan is like US but with Japanese language"
    - Reality: Buying process, customer preference, regulatory are all different
    Score: 3/9 (high risk, low confidence)

─────────────────────────────────────────────

SYNTHESIS:

Average score: 4.8/9 = BELOW THRESHOLD

Consensus concerns:
  - Unit economics are tight; not ideal for new market
  - Execution risk is high (wrong team)
  - Market is very competitive
  - Entry approach (direct sales) is not optimal

Panel verdict:
  5 experts: 1 Pass (none), 2 Conditional, 2 Reject

RECOMMENDATION: CONDITIONAL PASS
  Must address before proceeding:
  1. Hire in-country Country Manager with proven Japan track record
  2. Model unit economics for Japan market (higher CAC)
  3. Evaluate partnership model vs. direct sales (lower capital, faster entry)
  4. Understand competitive response from Salesforce (assume aggressive)

Re-review after conditions are met.
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
```

## How to Use This Framework

1. **Before executing a growth strategy:** Run through panel review (even if just internally asking expert questions)
2. **Quarterly:** Review live initiatives with panel lens (which experts would flag concerns?)
3. **After strategy failure:** Conduct post-mortem: Which expert would have caught this? Why didn't we listen?
4. **When priorities conflict:** Different experts prioritize differently; synthesize their views for balanced decision

The goal is not consensus (experts disagree). The goal is to hear all the perspectives that would matter to someone executing this strategy.
