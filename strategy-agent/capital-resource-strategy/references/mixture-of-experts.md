# Capital Strategy: Mixture of Experts

Before finalizing capital allocation decisions, stress-test them through the lens of 5 expert perspectives. Each expert sees blind spots the others miss. Together, they form a complete view.

---

## The 5 Experts

### 1. Warren Buffett Disciple (Capital Allocation Purist)

**Who:** Someone who has read Berkshire Hathaway's annual letters, understands ROIC deeply, and is skeptical of financial engineering.

**Review Lens:**
- ROIC is the only scorecard. Everything else is noise or accounting gimmickry.
- Cost of capital obsession. If ROIC doesn't exceed WACC + margin of safety, the deal is bad.
- Long time horizons. Willing to wait years for the right investment because compounding is powerful.
- Skeptical of "strategic" rationales that don't produce financial returns.
- Hates complexity. Simple businesses with durable competitive advantages beat complex structures.

**Key Questions:**
- What is the historical ROIC of this business? Is the acquired company's ROIC sustainable or does management get credit they don't deserve?
- Does the acquirer have a competitive advantage that actually improves returns, or are they overpaying for a commodity business?
- How much of the purchase price is paid for intangibles (goodwill)? If >50%, you're probably overpaying.
- What's the margin of safety? If this goes wrong, how much value is destroyed? If >20% of purchase price, too risky.
- Is this a business you'd want to own forever? If the answer is no, it's probably not worth buying.

**Scoring Rubric (0-20):**
- 18-20: Clear ROIC advantage, conservative valuation, long-term hold, proven business model
- 15-17: Good ROIC trajectory, reasonable valuation, clear competitive advantage
- 12-14: Acceptable ROIC, some execution risk, valuation defensible but not cheap
- 8-11: Below-hurdle ROIC or high execution risk; strategic rationale doesn't overcome financial weakness
- 0-7: Value destruction likely; should not proceed

---

### 2. Growth CFO (Scaling Expert)

**Who:** Has scaled multiple companies through venture rounds and rapid growth, understands when to be aggressive vs. conservative with capital.

**Review Lens:**
- Growth rate matters. In some contexts, growth beats profitability in the short term because optionality is valuable.
- Capital efficiency is non-negotiable. You can burn cash if you're acquiring customers with LTV > 3x CAC.
- Market timing matters. First-mover advantage is real. Sometimes paying premium to get there first is worth it.
- Optionality is huge. Small bets that enable big moves are worth doing even with low near-term ROIC.
- Organization scalability is critical. Can this team grow 10x? If not, ROIC won't matter if you hit the ceiling.

**Key Questions:**
- What's the customer acquisition cost? What's the lifetime value? Is the ratio healthy (LTV:CAC > 3:1)?
- How is growth rate trending? Accelerating, flat, decelerating? Why?
- What's the total addressable market (TAM)? If TAM is limited, no amount of capital unlocks scale.
- Can the management team scale? Have they grown a business 5x+ before? If not, execution risk is high.
- What's the competitive response? Will entrenched players fight back if we take share aggressively?
- Is there a platform play hidden here? Can this become a 10x business if we take short-term ROIC hit?

**Scoring Rubric (0-20):**
- 18-20: Exceptional growth potential, proven team, TAM is large, competitive moat forming, valuation reasonable given upside
- 15-17: Strong growth trajectory, TAM is large, team has experience, defensible position
- 12-14: Solid growth opportunity, execution risk present, TAM is decent, valuation is fair for growth profile
- 8-11: Growth is slowing or market is saturated; team execution questions; valuation doesn't justify risk
- 0-7: Market is small, team is unproven, or growth is peaking; capital allocated here is wasted

---

### 3. Activist Investor (Returns Ruthlessness)

**Who:** Has pushed boards to cut costs, shrink businesses, return cash to shareholders. Relentless focus on cash generation and shareholder return.

**Review Lens:**
- Capital discipline is everything. Every dollar spent is a dollar not returned to shareholders.
- Waste is enemy #1. Headcount growth, scope creep, "strategic" spending — all need to be justified with hard numbers.
- Cash flow matters more than EBIT. Can you convert earnings to cash? If not, something's wrong.
- Return excess capital now. Don't assume you'll generate future opportunities that justify hoarding cash.
- Reallocation is brutal. If business earns below WACC, sell it to someone else or shut it down. Don't let it fester.

**Key Questions:**
- What's the free cash flow (not EBIT) from this investment? Can you actually get the cash out, or is it trapped?
- How much cash flow is reinvested for maintenance vs. growth? If maintenance capex is growing faster than revenue, ROIC is declining.
- What's the payback period? If >5 years, you're betting on assumptions extending too far into the future.
- How much cost reduction is possible? Is there fat that can be cut without destroying returns?
- Can we return capital to shareholders instead? Dividend, buyback, or debt paydown. Why this investment over shareholder return?
- What's the disciplined exit plan? When will this stop earning above WACC? When will you sell it?

**Scoring Rubric (0-20):**
- 18-20: Strong cash generation, rapid payback (<3 years), clear exit path, mature business ready to harvest
- 15-17: Solid cash generation, payback <4 years, path to exit or harvest defined
- 12-14: Acceptable cash generation, payback <5 years, but some assumptions are optimistic
- 8-11: Weak cash generation or long payback period; cash is trapped in capex/working capital; exit plan unclear
- 0-7: Destroys cash flow, perpetual capex requirement, no clear path to return cash; should be exited or harvested

---

### 4. Treasury & Risk Manager (Downside Protection)

**Who:** Manages liquidity, stress-tests the balance sheet, thinks about tail risk and what happens if everything goes wrong.

**Review Lens:**
- Downside protection is paramount. What happens in a recession? Can we still service debt? Do we have cash cushion?
- Liquidity is survival. You can be profitable and die if you run out of cash.
- Stress testing is non-negotiable. Model the investment in a downturn scenario. If returns are negative, be very careful.
- Covenant headroom matters. Are we so tight on debt covenants that one bad quarter breaches them?
- Currency and interest rate risk. In a rising-rate environment, is our debt cost exploding?
- Concentration risk. Are we betting everything on one customer, one market, one product? Diversification is cheap insurance.

**Key Questions:**
- Model this investment in a recession (EBIT down 30-40%, revenue down 20-30%). Are returns still positive?
- What if working capital needs spike (bad). Can we absorb it without covenant breach?
- What if the customer base becomes concentrated (worst case: top 3 customers are 70% of revenue)? Mitigatable?
- Interest rate sensitivity. If rates rise 200 basis points, does our debt service spike? By how much?
- Refinancing risk. When does major debt mature? Do we have backup funding sources if credit markets seize?
- Exit optionality. If this goes bad, can we sell the business? Or are we stuck with it?

**Scoring Rubric (0-20):**
- 18-20: Resilient to downside scenarios, strong covenant cushion, diversified customer base, exit options exist
- 15-17: Withstands moderate downturn, adequate covenant cushion, reasonable customer concentration
- 12-14: Vulnerable to severe downturn, tight covenant cushion, some customer concentration, exit options limited
- 8-11: Downside scenario is painful, covenant breach risk if EBIT declines, high concentration, hard to exit
- 0-7: Downside is catastrophic, covenant violation likely in recession, concentrated customer base, stranded capital

---

### 5. Board Finance Committee Chair (Governance & Fiduciary)

**Who:** Sits on board, responsible for capital stewardship, cares about transparency and stakeholder balance.

**Review Lens:**
- Governance is foundational. Is capital allocation process transparent, consistent, and accountable?
- Shareholder alignment. Are we allocating capital to maximize shareholder value, or have politics taken over?
- Disclosure. Can we defend this decision to shareholders, regulators, and public? Is anything hidden?
- Stakeholder balance. Are we being fair to employees (layoffs?), customers (price increases?), communities (exit?)?
- Strategic coherence. Does this investment fit the strategy? Or is it a one-off that confuses the narrative?
- Board confidence. Do I trust this CEO and team to execute this? Have they delivered on past investments?

**Key Questions:**
- Is this decision consistent with the communicated strategy? Or does it surprise shareholders?
- Did the Capital Allocation Committee debate this thoroughly? Or is it a CEO power play?
- Have we been transparent about risks? Would shareholders approve this if they knew the risks?
- Who's accountable if this goes wrong? Is accountability clear?
- How does this affect employees, customers, communities? Are there externalities we're not accounting for?
- Does the team have a track record of hitting commitments? Or is this a high-execution-risk investment from a team with misses?
- Are we being fair to long-term shareholders vs. short-term stock price? Is this too aggressive or too cautious?

**Scoring Rubric (0-20):**
- 18-20: Transparent, strategically coherent, team has strong track record, board is confident in execution, stakeholder impact is positive
- 15-17: Clear rationale, fits strategy, team capable, acceptable stakeholder impact
- 12-14: Defensible decision, fits general strategy, team has mixed track record, some stakeholder concerns
- 8-11: Questionable transparency, weak connection to strategy, team execution concerns, governance process was weak
- 0-7: Opaque decision-making, inconsistent with strategy, team unproven or has poor track record, governance process failed

## Expert Panel Synthesis Protocol

### Run Each Expert Lens Independently

Don't let experts influence each other upfront. Each scores the investment separately (0-20) using their own rubric.

### Aggregate Scores

| Expert | Score | Key Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Buffett Disciple | [X]/20 | [Main critique] |
| Growth CFO | [X]/20 | [Main critique] |
| Activist Investor | [X]/20 | [Main critique] |
| Treasury/Risk | [X]/20 | [Main critique] |
| Board Finance Chair | [X]/20 | [Main critique] |
| **Average** | **[X]/20** | |

### Interpret the Score Distribution

**Narrow distribution (all experts score 16-20 or all score 4-8):**
- Consensus. Investment is clearly strong or clearly weak. Easy decision.

**Wide distribution (some score 18+, others score 6-):**
- Tension. Different experts see different aspects. Requires deliberation.

**Example interpretation:**
- Buffett scores 8 (ROIC is mediocre), Growth CFO scores 18 (market opportunity is huge), Activist scores 4 (terrible cash generation)
- Interpretation: This is a growth bet with poor near-term returns and questionable capital efficiency. Acceptable if you believe in the market opportunity and can afford the cash burn.

### Deliberation Process

**If consensus (tight distribution):**
- 16+: Approve and move quickly
- 6-: Reject and move on
- No extended debate needed

**If tension (wide distribution):**
- Identify the crux: What specific concern divides the experts?
- Assign one expert to debate: Buffett vs. Growth CFO on ROIC vs. TAM, or Activist vs. Growth CFO on payback vs. scale
- Decision rule: If 3+ experts score below 12, there's a material risk that requires mitigation. Design mitigation or kill.
- If only 1 expert scores low, understand why. Is it a blind spot of theirs or a real risk?

### Veto Rules (Hard Stops)

Certain expert objections are veto-worthy:

**Buffett Disciple veto:** ROIC is <WACC - 5% AND we have clear visibility that it won't improve. This is destroying value. Kill it.

**Treasury/Risk veto:** Downside scenario (recession) creates covenant breach risk AND we have no mitigation (no liquidity buffer, no exit option). Kill it.

**Board Finance veto:** Process was fundamentally flawed (CEO made decision alone, no real Capital Committee review, governance was violated) OR this decision is strategically incoherent (contradicts communicated strategy with no explanation). Kill it and re-do the process.

**2-expert veto rule:** If 2+ experts score <6, the investment has fundamental problems. Don't proceed without major redesign.

## When Expert Panels Disagree: Resolution

### Scenario 1: Buffett (6) vs. Growth CFO (18)

**Issue:** Buffett thinks ROIC is mediocre (8%). Growth CFO thinks market opportunity justifies the investment.

**Reconciliation:**
1. Affirm: Both perspectives are valid. ROIC matters for mature businesses. Market timing matters for growth.
2. Decision rule: If market TAM is >$[X]B and we're early (market <$[Y]B penetrated), growth CFO's view has weight.
3. Mitigations:
   - Set ROIC improvement target for Year 3-5
   - Build in kill trigger: If ROIC hasn't improved to [Z]% by Year 4, exit
   - Stage the investment: Small bet now, scale later if metrics improve
4. Decision: Proceed with conditional approval. ROIC must improve or we exit.

### Scenario 2: Activist Investor (4) vs. Growth CFO (16)

**Issue:** Activist hates cash burn. Growth CFO thinks burn rate is acceptable for market opportunity.

**Reconciliation:**
1. Affirm: Activist is right that payback is long (6+ years). Growth CFO is right that market is big.
2. Decision rule: What's the cash conversion cycle? When does this become cash-generative?
3. Mitigations:
   - Reduce burn rate by 20% through cost discipline
   - Accelerate revenue by improving sales efficiency
   - Set cash flow positive target: Must achieve positive operating cash flow by Year [X]
4. Decision: Proceed if burn rate is reduced and cash flow timeline is accelerated.

### Scenario 3: Treasury/Risk (3) vs. CEO (All others, 16+)

**Issue:** Treasury thinks downside is catastrophic. Everyone else is confident.

**Reconciliation:**
1. Take Treasury seriously. Downside protection is not paranoia.
2. Run actual downside scenario: Model this investment if EBIT declines 40%.
3. Mitigations:
   - Build in covenant headroom: Maintain debt/EBITDA <[X]x even if EBIT drops 40%
   - Keep war chest: Maintain $[M] in cash for contingency
   - Exit option: Structure deal with divestiture option if synergies miss
4. Decision: Proceed if downside scenarios are stress-tested and mitigations are built in.

## Hard Stops: When to Reject Despite Expert Consensus

Even if experts broadly approve (say, average score of 16+), there are red flags that warrant rejection:

- **Illegal or unethical:** No score overrides law or ethics.
- **Destroys culture:** Investment requires laying off half the company and demoralizing the other half. Cost-benefit is negative.
- **Creates enterprise risk:** Single acquisition would mean 60% of revenue is one customer. Unacceptable concentration.
- **Violates stated strategy:** Board communicated we're profitable and disciplined. This investment signals the opposite. Confuses stakeholders.
- **Team is unproven:** Acquired CEO has pattern of overpromising and underdelivering. Execution risk is too high.
- **Governance was broken:** CFO was bypassed, board wasn't consulted, decision was made in secret. Process failure doesn't produce good decisions.

## Expert Panel Best Practices

- [ ] All 5 expert lenses are applied to major investments (>$[X]M)
- [ ] Experts score independently before discussing (no anchoring)
- [ ] Score distribution is analyzed for consensus vs. tension
- [ ] Wide distributions trigger deliberation on key disagreements
- [ ] Veto rules are pre-defined and respected
- [ ] Resolution process is transparent (how tensions were resolved)
- [ ] Final decision is documented with scoring rationale
- [ ] 2-expert veto rule is enforced (if 2+ experts score <6, kill or redesign)
- [ ] Panel scores feed back into lessons learned (are our experts calibrated correctly?)
