# Funding Strategy Framework

Funding strategy answers: How do we finance our capital plan? What mix of debt, equity, and retained earnings optimizes our balance sheet? When should we raise capital, how much, and at what stage?

Funding strategy is distinct from capital allocation. Capital allocation asks "where does capital go?" Funding strategy asks "where does capital come from?"

---

## Core Principle: Match Funding to Life Cycle

Different company stages have different optimal capital structures and funding sources.

| Stage | Capital Needs | Optimal Funding | Debt Capacity | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Startup** | High burn, negative cash flow | Founder capital, angel, VC | None (pre-revenue) | 18-24 months between rounds |
| **Growth** | High growth capex, expanding working capital | VC, Series A/B, retain earnings | Minimal (high burn) | 18 months between rounds |
| **Scale** | Continued growth capex, working capital management | Late-stage VC, growth equity, debt | Moderate (cash flow positive) | 12-18 months |
| **Mature** | Maintenance capex, growth capex in pockets | Retained earnings, debt, strategic equity | High (stable cash flow) | Continuous |
| **Turnaround** | Recovery capex, debt refinancing | Equity (dilutive but necessary), restructured debt | Low (stressed cash flow) | Varies |

## Step 2: Retained Earnings Strategy

**Retained earnings are your cheapest, most flexible source of capital.** No dilution. No covenants. No fixed obligations. But limited by operating performance.

### Can You Self-Fund Growth?

**Formula:** Operating Cash Flow ÷ Total Capital Needs = Self-funding %

Examples:
- Mature business: $100M operating cash flow ÷ $50M capital needs = 200% self-funded (can return to shareholders)
- Growth business: $30M operating cash flow ÷ $100M capital needs = 30% self-funded (need 70% external capital)
- Startup: $0M operating cash flow ÷ $10M capital needs = 0% self-funded (100% external capital needed)

### Earnings Retention Policy

**Conservative approach:** Retain earnings sufficient to fund:
- Maintenance capex + working capital growth
- 10-20% growth capex (modest expansion)
- Keep debt/EBITDA ratio within target range

**Distribute:** Dividends, share buybacks, or debt paydown with excess earnings

**Aggressive approach:** Reinvest all earnings in growth capex
- Maximizes growth rate
- Minimizes shareholder distributions
- Increases financial risk if growth slows

**Middle ground:** Retain earnings to fund growth targets; distribute excess as dividend or buyback

**Example policy:**
```
Earnings Retention Policy
═══════════════════════════════════════
1. Retain earnings sufficient for maintenance capex + 50% of planned growth capex
2. If debt/EBITDA > 2.5x, retain additional earnings to pay down debt
3. If debt/EBITDA < 2.0x AND growth capex is funded, distribute 30-50% as dividend
4. Any strategic M&A opportunity ≥ $[X]M → retain earnings to build dry powder
═══════════════════════════════════════
```

## Step 3: Debt Financing Strategy

**Debt is efficient capital.** Interest is tax-deductible. Leverage amplifies returns if ROIC > cost of debt. But debt creates fixed obligations and financial risk.

### Debt Capacity Analysis

**Key metrics:**

- **Interest Coverage Ratio** = EBIT ÷ Interest Expense
  - Target: ≥ 2.5x in base case, ≥ 1.5x in downturn
  - If ICR < 2.0x, you're stretching financial risk

- **Debt/EBITDA Ratio** = Total Debt ÷ EBITDA
  - Target by credit rating:
    - A-rated: ≤ 2.0x
    - BBB-rated (investment grade): ≤ 3.0x
    - BB-rated (below investment grade): ≤ 4.0x
  - Higher leverage = higher risk, higher borrowing costs

- **Debt/Equity Ratio** = Total Debt ÷ Total Equity
  - Target: 30-50% for stable businesses, 10-30% for volatile/growth

### Stress Test Your Debt Capacity

**Recession scenario:** Model EBIT down 30-40%, revenue down 20-30%

```
Base Case:
  EBIT: $100M
  Interest Expense: $20M
  Interest Coverage: 5.0x
  Debt/EBITDA: 2.5x

Recession (EBIT -30%, but debt amount unchanged):
  EBIT: $70M
  Interest Expense: $20M
  Interest Coverage: 3.5x
  Debt/EBITDA: 3.6x

Can you service debt in recession? Yes, but margin is tight.
Recommendation: Don't go above 2.0x debt/EBITDA if interested coverage would fall below 2.0x in recession.
```

### When to Raise Debt

**Favorable conditions for debt financing:**
- Rates are at historic lows relative to your cost
- Your credit spread is tight (investors want your debt)
- EBIT is growing (debt service is becoming easier)
- Cash flows are stable and forecastable
- Asset base is growing (more collateral)

**Avoid debt financing when:**
- Rates are rising and your spread is widening
- EBIT is declining or volatile
- Refinancing risk is near (existing debt matures soon)
- Cash flows are unpredictable
- Balance sheet is already levered

### Debt Structure: Term Loans vs. Bonds vs. Revolving Credit

| Type | Use Case | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Revolving Credit Facility (RCF)** | Working capital flexibility, bridge financing | Flexible draw/repay, cheap until drawn | Covenants, commitment fees |
| **Term Loan** | Growth capex, M&A, debt refinancing | Longer tenor (5-7 yrs), amortizing, fixed rate | Less flexible, prepayment penalties |
| **Bonds** | Large capital raises, long-dated financing | Public markets, no bank relationship, 10-30yr tenor | Costly (rating, legal fees), less flexible |
| **Asset-Backed Lending** | Financing specific assets (real estate, equipment) | Asset-backed reduces lender risk | Requires collateral, limits flexibility |
| **Vendor Financing** | Working capital (payment terms from suppliers) | Cheap, no covenants | Relationship risk, could be withdrawn |

**Typical structure:**
- RCF: $[M] for working capital and contingencies
- Term Loan: $[M] for major capex/M&A
- Bonds: $[M] if capital need is >$100M+ and tenor is long

## Step 4: Equity Financing Strategy

**Equity financing is expensive.** You give up ownership, future earnings, and control. But it provides permanent capital with no repayment obligation.

### When to Raise Equity

**Raise equity when:**
- Your valuation is high (relative to historical or peers)
- You need permanent capital for a multi-year growth plan
- Your balance sheet is weak and you need to deleverage
- You're investing in a new market with binary outcomes (high risk)
- M&A opportunity is large and requires capital for contingencies
- You need to signal confidence to employees, customers, partners

**Avoid raising equity when:**
- Your valuation is low (relative to historical or peers)
- You can fund growth with debt at attractive rates
- Raising equity would dilute key shareholders materially
- You don't need the capital yet (waiting signals you have optionality)

### Equity Financing Decision Tree

```
EQUITY FINANCING DECISION
          │
          ├─ Do you need external capital?
          │  ├─ No → Don't raise. Retain earnings, focus on ROIC.
          │  │
          │  └─ Yes → Continue...
          │
          ├─ Can you fund with debt at acceptable rates?
          │  ├─ Yes (cost < 6%, debt/EBITDA < 3.0x) → Use debt instead. Equity is more expensive.
          │  │
          │  └─ No (rates high, leverage high, no debt appetite) → Consider equity...
          │
          ├─ Is your valuation attractive relative to intrinsic value?
          │  ├─ Yes (trading at >1.5x intrinsic) → Raise equity now. Shareholders won't get better offer later.
          │  │
          │  └─ No (trading near or below intrinsic) → Wait if possible. Only raise if capital need is urgent.
          │
          ├─ How much capital do you need?
          │  ├─ <$25M (small) → Raise from existing investors, strategic partners, or secondary financing
          │  ├─ $25-100M (medium) → Mix of institutional investors and strategic investors
          │  └─ >$100M (large) → IPO, secondary offering, or large growth equity fund
          │
          ├─ What dilution can you accept?
          │  ├─ <10% (founders want to maintain control) → Raise from aligned investors; may need lower valuation
          │  ├─ 10-20% (acceptable) → Standard for institutional rounds
          │  └─ >20% (significant) → Signals distress; only if capital is critical
          │
          └─ Close the round and execute.
```

### Equity Financing by Company Stage

**Startup (Pre-revenue):**
- Source: Founder capital, angel investors, early-stage VCs
- Amount: $[X]K to $[X]M
- Dilution: 10-20% per round
- Use: Product development, initial team, market validation
- Timeline: 18-24 months until Series A

**Growth (Revenue, not profitable):**
- Source: Series A/B/C VCs, growth equity
- Amount: $[X]M to $[X]M+
- Dilution: 15-25% per round
- Use: Sales & marketing, team scaling, geographic expansion, product development
- Timeline: 18-24 months between rounds

**Scale (Revenue growth, approaching profitability):**
- Source: Late-stage VCs, growth equity, pre-IPO rounds
- Amount: $[X]M to $[X]M+
- Dilution: 10-20%
- Use: Continued growth capex, working capital, strategic M&A, path to profitability
- Timeline: 12-18 months before IPO

**Mature (Profitable, stable cash flow):**
- Source: Rare to raise external equity; IPO or strategic equity if needed
- Amount: If raised, $[X]M+ for specific strategic use
- Dilution: Modest (public equity is fungible)
- Use: Strategic M&A, entering new markets, significant capex programs
- Timeline: Ongoing based on opportunity

## Step 5: Capital Structure Optimization

Your **target capital structure** balances three goals:
1. **Financial flexibility** — Ability to raise capital if needed
2. **Cost of capital minimization** — Debt is cheaper than equity (tax shield + lower required return)
3. **Financial stability** — Debt service is manageable even if EBIT declines

### Target Capital Structure by Industry

| Industry | Typical Debt/EBITDA | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer Goods / Packaged | 2.0-2.5x | Stable, predictable cash flows support leverage |
| Technology / Software | 0.5-1.5x | High growth, high volatility; lower debt tolerance |
| Utilities / Infrastructure | 3.0-4.0x | Highly predictable cash flows; high debt tolerance |
| Healthcare / Pharma | 1.5-2.5x | Stable but regulatory risk; moderate leverage |
| Real Estate | 3.5-4.5x | Asset-backed; leverage based on cash flow from assets |
| Manufacturing | 2.0-3.0x | Moderate stability; asset-backed lending available |
| Retail | 1.5-2.5x | Cyclical; leverage depends on brand strength |

### Optimize to Your Target

**Current state:** Debt/EBITDA = 1.5x, Target = 2.5x

**Action:** You have debt capacity. Options:
1. Raise debt for growth capex, retain the capital, increase leverage to 2.5x
2. Issue dividend/buyback, which reduces equity value, increases debt/equity ratio
3. Acquire a company with debt financing
4. All of the above

**Current state:** Debt/EBITDA = 3.5x, Target = 2.5x

**Action:** You're over-leveraged. Options:
1. Retain earnings to pay down debt (reduce capex or raise equity for growth instead)
2. Sell non-core assets, use proceeds to pay down debt
3. Equity raise to pay down debt (dilutive but deleverages quickly)
4. Wait for EBITDA to grow (denominator increases, ratio declines)

## Step 6: Working Capital Management

Working capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities. Poorly managed working capital can strangle cash flow even if you're profitable.

### Working Capital Cycle

```
Cash → Inventory → Receivables → Cash (again)

Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO): How long until inventory is sold?
Days Sales Outstanding (DSO): How long until customers pay?
Days Payable Outstanding (DPO): How long before you pay suppliers?

Cash Conversion Cycle = DIO + DSO - DPO

If CCC = 45 days: It takes 45 days for a dollar of inventory purchase to convert back to cash.
If you grow 50% and CCC = 45 days: You need 50% more cash tied up in working capital.
```

### Optimize Each Component

**Reduce Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO):**
- Forecast demand more accurately (less guess-and-check)
- Implement just-in-time inventory practices
- Liquidate obsolete inventory
- Use vendor-managed inventory for slow-moving items

**Reduce Days Sales Outstanding (DSO):**
- Tighten credit terms (net 30 instead of net 45)
- Offer early-pay discounts (1% off for payment in 10 days)
- Improve invoicing speed
- Follow up on collections faster
- Switch to payment-on-delivery for high-risk customers

**Increase Days Payable Outstanding (DPO):**
- Negotiate extended terms with suppliers (net 60 instead of net 30)
- Use supply chain financing programs
- Batch payments for efficiency
- Don't pay early unless there's a meaningful discount

**Example:** Reduce CCC from 45 days to 30 days

```
Current: $1B revenue, CCC = 45 days
  Working capital tied up = $1B × (45 / 365) = $123M

Optimized: $1B revenue, CCC = 30 days
  Working capital tied up = $1B × (30 / 365) = $82M

Cash freed up: $41M
Use of freed cash: Debt paydown, capex, dividend, or dry powder
```

## Worked Example: Scaling SaaS Company Funding Strategy

**Context:**
- Current revenue: $50M (growing 40% YoY)
- Current EBIT: $(5M) (investing in growth)
- Projected revenue in 3 years: $150M
- Want to reach profitability while continuing 30%+ growth
- Current cash position: $20M
- No debt

**Capital Needs (3-year forecast):**

| Need | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Growth headcount (sales, eng, CS) | $20M | $25M | $25M | $70M |
| Cloud infrastructure | $5M | $7M | $10M | $22M |
| Capex (office, equipment) | $3M | $2M | $2M | $7M |
| Working capital (growth in AR, prepaid) | $5M | $8M | $5M | $18M |
| **Total Needs** | **$33M** | **$42M** | **$42M** | **$117M** |

**Cash Sources (3-year forecast):**

| Source | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Operating cash flow (negative, investing heavily) | $(8M) | $(5M) | $10M | $(3M) |
| **Total Before External Funding** | **$(8M)** | **$(5M)** | **$10M** | **$(3M)** |

**Funding Gap:** $117M - $(3M) = $120M needed in external capital

**Funding Strategy:**

1. **Use existing cash:** $20M (don't deplete below $5-10M minimum)
2. **Raise equity:** $80M at attractive post-growth-surge valuation (you're still pre-profitability but have proven product-market fit)
   - Brings in growth equity investor
   - Signals confidence to market, customers, employees
   - Provides runway through profitability inflection
3. **Debt capacity:** Keep debt/revenue ratio low (you're high-growth). Only use if needed.
4. **Retain earnings:** As profitability improves in Year 3, begin retaining earnings instead of distributing.

**Resulting balance sheet:**
- Year 1: Cash $12M + raised $80M = $92M cash vs. $33M spend = $59M excess
- Year 2: Begin spending down; cash = $17M vs. continued needs
- Year 3: Profitable; begin retaining earnings

**Outcome:** Funded runway through profitability, retained control (growth equity is passive investor), positioned for next stage (debt, IPO, or dividend).

## Funding Strategy Best Practices Checklist

- [ ] Funding gap is calculated for next 3-5 years
- [ ] Operating cash flow forecast is conservative (revenue growth assumptions tested)
- [ ] Debt capacity is stress-tested in recession scenario (ICR stays ≥ 1.5x)
- [ ] Target capital structure is defined (debt/EBITDA, debt/equity, or industry benchmarks)
- [ ] Earnings retention policy is explicit (what % to retain vs. distribute)
- [ ] Equity financing triggers are defined (when to raise, at what valuation, how much dilution acceptable)
- [ ] Working capital cycle is optimized (DSO, DIO, DPO targets set)
- [ ] Cash flow forecast is updated quarterly
- [ ] Debt covenants are manageable (not too restrictive, have headroom)
- [ ] Refinancing risk is managed (stagger debt maturities, don't have wall of maturities at once)
- [ ] Currency and interest rate risk are hedged (if relevant)
- [ ] Minimum cash balance is defined and maintained
