# Capital Allocation Framework

Capital allocation is the discipline of deploying capital across competing uses to maximize return on invested capital (ROIC). Every dollar deployed to use A is not available for use B. This framework helps make that allocation trade-off explicitly and analytically.

## Core Principle: ROIC Obsession

Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) = EBIT × (1 - Tax Rate) / Invested Capital

ROIC is the universal scorecard. Not growth rate. Not market share. Not revenue. ROIC captures whether capital is being used productively.

**Key Thresholds:**
- ROIC > Cost of Capital (WACC): Creating value. Allocate more capital here.
- ROIC = Cost of Capital: Breaking even. Allocate only if strategic benefit exists.
- ROIC < Cost of Capital: Destroying value. Reallocate away.

**Golden Rule:** If a business earns ROIC below your cost of capital, scale it down or exit it, regardless of how strategically important it feels. Return capital to shareholders or redeploy to higher-ROIC uses. This is not callousness — it's math.

## Step 1: Current Allocation Assessment

### Map All Capital Recipients

Create a simple table of all material capital deployment:

| Business Unit / Segment | Invested Capital ($M) | Revenue ($M) | EBIT ($M) | ROIC (%) | Growth Rate (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core / Mature | 500 | 800 | 160 | 25% | 2% |
| Growth Unit | 200 | 150 | 15 | 6% | 40% |
| Emerging Market | 100 | 50 | 2 | 1.4% | 80% |
| Corporate / Overhead | 50 | - | (20) | (32%) | - |
| **Total** | **850** | **1,000** | **157** | **16%** | - |

**Key metrics to track:**
- Invested Capital = Total Assets - Non-interest-bearing Current Liabilities (approximation: you subtract suppliers payable, accrued expenses, but keep debt and equity financing)
- EBIT = Operating profit before interest and taxes
- Tax rate = Statutory or normalized rate (use 25% if uncertain)
- ROIC = EBIT × (1 - Tax) / Invested Capital

### Calculate ROIC Variance

**Benchmark against cost of capital:**
- If WACC = 8%, then:
  - Core Unit (25% ROIC) is creating $850M × (25% - 8%) = $144M of value
  - Growth Unit (6% ROIC) is destroying $200M × (6% - 8%) = $4M of value
  - Emerging Market (1.4% ROIC) is destroying $100M × (1.4% - 8%) = $6.6M of value
  - Corporate overhead is pure cost

**Total value destruction:** $10.6M annually from Growth and Emerging segments alone.

## Step 2: Identify Reallocation Opportunities

### Quadrant Analysis: ROIC vs. Growth

```
              HIGH ROIC
                 ▲
                 │   WINNERS              MATURE CASH COWS
                 │   (High ROIC,          (High ROIC,
                 │    High Growth)         Low Growth)
                 │
                 │   • Allocate more      • Harvest
                 │   • Scale              • Use for funding
                 │   • Defend              • Protect against
                 │                          disruption
        ────────┼──────────────────────────────────────►
                 │                        LOW ROIC
                 │
                 │   TURNAROUNDS          PROBLEM CHILDREN
      LOW ROIC   │   (Low ROIC,           (Low ROIC,
                 │    High Growth)         Low Growth)
                 │
                 │   • Fix or exit        • Exit or harvest
                 │   • Invest if          • Reduce capex
                 │     fixable
```

### Diagnostic Questions

For each low-ROIC segment:

1. **Is this a temporary valley or permanent problem?**
   - Temporary: Early in growth cycle, investing ahead of returns, industry slowdown, temporary capex spike
   - Permanent: Structural cost disadvantage, pricing pressure from competition, market shrinking, business model broken

2. **Can we fix it with operational improvement?**
   - Can we cut costs? (Reduce headcount, consolidate footprint, improve procurement)
   - Can we raise prices? (Differentiate, shift to higher-margin products, move upmarket)
   - Can we improve asset utilization? (Run plants at higher capacity, reduce excess inventory, improve turns)
   - If yes to any: invest in turnaround. If no: consider exit.

3. **What's the opportunity cost?**
   - How much invested capital is tied up in this segment?
   - If reallocated to a 12% ROIC opportunity, what's the annual value creation?
   - Example: Move $100M from 4% ROIC to 12% ROIC segment = $8M annual additional value creation

4. **What are the political / organizational costs of reallocation?**
   - Will exit destroy important customer relationships?
   - Will it cut jobs in key locations?
   - Will it demoralize remaining business units?
   - These are real costs, but they don't override ROIC discipline. They argue for *how* you exit (gradual vs. fast, sale vs. shutdown), not *whether*.

### Reallocation Priority Ranking

1. **Tier 1: Must Exit or Severely Downsize**
   - ROIC < WACC - 3% and no clear path to improvement
   - Destroying value despite management attention
   - Capital could earn 10+ percentage points more elsewhere
   - **Action:** Divest within 12 months or reduce capex by 50%

2. **Tier 2: Turnaround or Harvest**
   - ROIC < WACC but fixable with management focus
   - OR high-growth but low-ROIC with clear path to profitability
   - **Action:** Assign turnaround leader, set 18-month improvement target, kill if not achieved

3. **Tier 3: Protect and Defend**
   - ROIC ≈ WACC or slightly above, strategically important
   - Competitors are circling or disruption is threat
   - **Action:** Maintain capex to defend position, don't starve, but don't expand

4. **Tier 4: Invest and Scale**
   - ROIC > WACC + 5%, growing market, competitive advantage
   - **Action:** Increase capex, protect talent, capture market share

## Step 3: Decompose ROIC to Find Levers

ROIC = (EBIT Margin) × (Asset Turnover)

This shows you WHERE to improve a low-ROIC business:

| Segment | EBIT Margin | Asset Turnover | ROIC | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core | 20% | 1.25 | 25% | Strong |
| Growth | 10% | 0.6 | 6% | Both low — unprofitable AND capital intensive |
| Emerging | 4% | 0.35 | 1.4% | Capital intensity killer |

**Interpretation:**
- **Growth** has two problems: margins are half of Core, and it's capital-intensive. Need to focus on both.
  - Can we improve margins? (Price, cost, mix)
  - Can we reduce capital intensity? (Outsource, lease vs. own, asset-light model)
- **Emerging** is the worst: margins are negligible, capital intensity is extreme.
  - This looks like a startup's unit economics (high burn, high capex, high growth). Fine if it's early. Problematic if it's mature.

## Step 4: The Reallocation Decision Tree

```
LOW-ROIC SEGMENT DISCOVERED
          │
          ├─ Temporary or permanent?
          │  ├─ Temporary (early cycle, capex spike, industry reset)
          │  │  └─ Action: Hold. Review in 12 months.
          │  │
          │  └─ Permanent (structural disadvantage)
          │     └─ Continue below...
          │
          ├─ Can we fix it?
          │  ├─ Yes (clear operational lever)
          │  │  ├─ Is fix worth the effort?
          │  │  │  ├─ Yes (payoff is $[M] annually)
          │  │  │  │  └─ Action: Assign turnaround leader, 18-month target, kill if not hit
          │  │  │  │
          │  │  │  └─ No (payoff is $[M], too small relative to effort)
          │  │  │     └─ Action: Skip turnaround, go to harvest
          │  │  │
          │  │  └─ No (broken business model, structural cost disadvantage, competitor advantage is unbridgeable)
          │  │     └─ Continue below...
          │  │
          │  └─ Strategic value?
          │     ├─ Yes (enables market, blocks competitor, required for ecosystem)
          │     │  ├─ How much capital required?
          │     │  │  ├─ $[M] — affordable; maintain strategic investment at reduced ROIC threshold
          │     │  │  │
          │     │  │  └─ $[M] — too much relative to strategic value; exit
          │     │  │
          │     │  └─ No (financial investment only, no strategic rationale)
          │     │     └─ Action: Exit (sell, shutdown, or harvest)
          │
          └─ If harvesting or exiting:
             ├─ Can we sell it?
             │  ├─ Yes (buyer exists who sees value)
             │  │  └─ Action: Divest at best price
             │  │
             │  └─ No (no buyer, or buyer valuations are terrible)
             │     ├─ Liquidate?
             │     │  ├─ Yes (can recover most of asset value)
             │     │  │  └─ Action: Shut down, liquidate assets, redeploy proceeds
             │     │  │
             │     │  └─ No (assets are stranded, no secondary market)
             │     │     └─ Action: Harvest (cut capex, extract cash, let it decline)
```

## Anti-Patterns & What They Cost

### Anti-Pattern 1: Peanut Butter Spreading

**Definition:** Spreading capital evenly across all business units regardless of ROIC. "Every division gets 5% growth in capex budget."

**Why it happens:**
- Fairness logic (everyone gets their piece)
- Political compromise (can't play favorites)
- Lazy (easier to use a formula than judge each unit)

**What it costs:**
- Capital flows to low-ROIC segments by default
- High-ROIC segments get starved of growth capital
- Total company ROIC declines over time
- Competitors who allocate disciplined to winners pull away in value creation

**Example:**
```
Peanut Butter Allocation:
  Core (25% ROIC): $500M → add $25M (growth: 5%)
  Growth (6% ROIC): $200M → add $10M (growth: 5%)
  Emerging (1.4% ROIC): $100M → add $5M (growth: 5%)

Value Created: ($25M × 17%) + ($10M × -2%) + ($5M × -6.6%) = $3.8M
Total ROIC: 15.5% (down from 16%)

Disciplined Allocation:
  Core (25% ROIC): $500M → add $0M (hold)
  Growth (6% ROIC): $200M → add $0M (harvest, don't grow)
  Emerging (1.4% ROIC): $100M → reallocate $50M to Core

Value Created: ($50M × 17%) = $8.5M
Total ROIC: 17.2% (up from 16%)

Difference: $8.5M - $3.8M = $4.7M annual value creation
Over 10 years: $47M+ value gap
```

**How to fix it:**
- Allocate capital based on ROIC ranking, not history or size
- Set allocation formula: "Capital goes to ROIC > [hurdle], scaled by opportunity size"
- Publish the rankings; make it transparent who gets capital and why
- Accept that some divisions shrink; that's how capital discipline works

### Anti-Pattern 2: Sunk Cost Anchoring

**Definition:** Continuing to fund investments because "we've already spent so much." Past capital is sunk; it's gone. Only forward returns matter.

**Why it happens:**
- Behavioral anchoring (the $100M already invested "anchors" the decision)
- Avoiding admitting past failure ("We've invested in this; it must work eventually")
- Fear of writing down assets on the balance sheet

**What it costs:**
- Throwing good money after bad
- Tying up capital that should be redeployed
- Years of below-ROIC returns before finally exiting
- Competitors kill low-ROIC initiatives faster, reallocate, and overtake you

**Example:**
```
$200M investment made 4 years ago in emerging market.
  Current ROIC: 1.4%
  Sunk cost: $200M (irrelevant)
  Forward-looking incremental ROIC: -2% (market shrinking, competition intensifying)

Sunk Cost Logic:
  "We've invested $200M; we need to give it 2 more years to break even"
  → Additional $40M capex deployed
  → Another 2 years of -2% returns = -$1.6M value destruction
  → Then exit with $180M recovered (write-down of $20M)

Disciplined Logic:
  "We're earning -2% on our $200M going forward. Cost of capital is 8%."
  → This will never improve (markets, competition, cost structure are structural)
  → Exit now. Sell for $200M or liquidate for $180M.
  → Reallocate $200M to 12% ROIC opportunity
  → Annual value creation: $200M × 4% improvement = $8M/year
  → Difference over 2 years: $8M - (-$1.6M) = $9.6M
```

**How to fix it:**
- Explicitly separate sunk costs (past) from marginal returns (future)
- Frame every investment decision forward-looking: "If we were making this decision today, knowing what we know now, what would we do?"
- Kill underperformers fast. ROIC discipline means you exit before the catastrophic loss, not after.
- Create a "kill decision" process separate from approval. Make it as rigorous to exit as to enter.

### Anti-Pattern 3: Political Allocation

**Definition:** Capital allocation driven by who has the loudest voice, best relationship with the CEO, or most powerful faction on the board — not by ROIC.

**Why it happens:**
- No transparent decision-making process
- Capital allocation power is centralized with one person's judgment
- ROIC discipline is seen as "too rigid" by people with stakes in underperforming units
- "Strategic importance" becomes undefined code for "I want it"

**What it costs:**
- Capital systematically flows away from winners, toward entrenched interests
- Decision-makers optimize for their empire, not shareholder returns
- Company-wide ROIC declines; rivals pull ahead
- Smart leaders exit for companies with better capital discipline

**Example:**
```
Company has three divisions. CFO wants to cut capex in mature division (25% ROIC),
reallocate to growth division (6% ROIC) with a visionary new leader.

Political Allocation:
  New leader is charismatic, board loves him, CEO is backing him
  → Approve $50M capex for growth division
  → Redirect $50M from mature division
  → ROIC of growth division remains 6% (problems aren't solved by more capital)
  → Mature division (25% ROIC) now constrained, growth slows
  → Company ROIC declines
  → In 3 years, growth division still hasn't proven itself; $50M has been wasted
  → Mature division's continued decline has created a margin problem

Disciplined Allocation:
  Growth division must clear 10% ROIC hurdle to get capital
  → Currently 6% ROIC; needs to fix underlying cost or margin problem first
  → Allocate $[M] to diagnostic and turnaround, not growth capex
  → Mature division (25% ROIC) gets growth capital; it's the high-ROIC business
  → Result: disciplined capital, predictable returns, strategic alignment to winners
```

**How to fix it:**
- Publish decision-making rules *before* capital requests come in (not after)
- Build a capital allocation committee with diverse representation; no single person controls allocation
- Tie executive compensation to ROIC metrics, not to their division's growth
- Require formal investment theses for anything material
- Review actual vs. thesis ROIC quarterly; make misses a big deal

## Worked Example: Capital Reallocation for a $1B Diversified Company

### Current State

| Division | Revenue | EBIT | EBIT Margin | Invested Capital | ROIC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Industrial Products | $500M | $100M | 20% | $300M | 26.7% |
| Software & Services | $300M | $45M | 15% | $150M | 20% |
| Legacy Chemical | $150M | $12M | 8% | $250M | 3.2% |
| Real Estate / Infrastructure | $50M | $2.5M | 5% | $100M | 1.7% |
| **Total** | **$1,000M** | **$159.5M** | **15.9%** | **$800M** | **13.2%** |

Assume WACC = 8% and tax rate = 25%.

### Diagnostics

**Value destruction by segment (vs. 8% WACC):**
- Industrial Products: ($300M × (26.7% - 8%)) = $56.1M value created
- Software & Services: ($150M × (20% - 8%)) = $18M value created
- Legacy Chemical: ($250M × (3.2% - 8%)) = -$12M value destroyed
- Real Estate: ($100M × (1.7% - 8%)) = -$6.3M value destroyed
- **Total:** $55.8M value created, but $18.3M destroyed by two segments

**Reallocation opportunity:**
- Legacy Chemical and Real Estate combined: $350M invested capital, 2.5% ROIC
- If reallocated to Software (20% ROIC): $350M × (20% - 2.5%) = $6.125M annual value creation
- If reallocated to Industrial (26.7% ROIC): $350M × (26.7% - 2.5%) = $8.47M annual value creation

### Action Plan

**Immediate (0-6 months):**
1. Legacy Chemical: Hire turnaround leader. Diagnostic: Is this a margin problem? Cost problem? Pricing problem?
   - If fixable, commit $[M] to turnaround with 18-month improvement target
   - If not fixable, begin divestiture process

2. Real Estate: Review strategic value. Does it:
   - Generate real estate financing for operating divisions? (If yes, value it correctly; don't separate it)
   - Stand alone as real estate play? (If yes, ROIC is 1.7%; bad. Divest.)

**Medium-term (6-18 months):**
3. Assuming Legacy Chemical isn't turning around and Real Estate isn't strategic:
   - Begin sale of both. Target buyers: private equity, competitors, real estate investors
   - Forecast: Sell Legacy Chemical for $150M (2/3 of invested capital)
   - Forecast: Sell Real Estate for $75M (3/4 of invested capital)
   - Total proceeds: $225M (plus retained earnings of ~$40M = $265M available)

4. Redeploy:
   - $100M to Software (highest ROIC, growth potential)
   - $100M to Industrial (defend and grow market leader)
   - $65M retained as war chest / debt paydown

**Projected result (2 years post-reallocation):**
- Industrial: $400M invested capital, 27% ROIC = $27.6M annual EBIT creation
- Software: $250M invested capital, 20% ROIC = $18M annual EBIT creation (higher from scale)
- No Legacy Chemical or Real Estate drag
- **New company ROIC: ~22%** (vs. 13.2% today)
- **Annual value creation vs. status quo: $225M × 14% = $31.5M additional ROIC**

## Capital Allocation Best Practices Checklist

- [ ] **ROIC is calculated and tracked** by segment, quarterly
- [ ] **WACC is defined** and reviewed annually (updated for capital structure changes)
- [ ] **Hurdle rates are published** before capital requests; they're not post-hoc rationalizations
- [ ] **Low-ROIC segments are identified** and action plans assigned (fix, harvest, exit)
- [ ] **Reallocation decision tree is documented** and used consistently
- [ ] **Sunk costs are explicitly excluded** from go/no-go decisions
- [ ] **Strategic investments** (below WACC hurdle) are capped at % of total capital and tied to strategic benefit metrics
- [ ] **Capital allocation committee meets quarterly** and reviews realized vs. thesis ROIC
- [ ] **Executive compensation is tied** to ROIC improvement, not just revenue growth
- [ ] **Board is updated monthly/quarterly** on actual vs. budget ROIC by segment
- [ ] **Kill triggers are pre-defined:** "If ROIC < [X]% for 2 consecutive quarters, automatic review"
- [ ] **Transparency:** Rankings of units by ROIC are published; everyone knows who's getting capital and why
