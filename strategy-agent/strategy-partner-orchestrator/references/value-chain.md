# Value Chain & Value Capture Analysis (10x Version)

## What Changed from the Original
The original used Porter's manufacturing-era value chain without adapting for services, platforms, or digital businesses. It also missed the financial rigor and defensibility analysis that turns insight into action. This version adds: activity-level cost modeling, linkage analysis (Porter's "fit" — how activities reinforce each other to create defensibility), digital transformation overlays, capability mapping, customer journey alignment, multi-business value chains, benchmarking methodology, and a worked example. The result is a framework a COO could use to redesign operating model architecture.

## When to Use
Use when redesigning operating models, making cost-reduction decisions, evaluating outsourcing, understanding competitive defensibility, or planning digital transformation. Particularly valuable for: cost transformation programs, make-vs-buy decisions, identifying where to invest for competitive advantage, and understanding where margin is actually captured (not just created).

## Step 1: Map the Value-Creating Activities

Choose the activity map that fits the business type:

### For Product / Manufacturing Businesses:
**Primary Activities:** Inbound logistics (sourcing, receiving, warehousing) | Operations (manufacturing, assembly, QC) | Outbound logistics (distribution, delivery, fulfillment) | Marketing & sales (brand, demand generation, sales) | Service (support, maintenance, returns)

### For Service Businesses:
**Primary Activities:** Business development (lead gen, proposals, contracting) | Service design (methodology, customization) | Service delivery (execution, project management) | Client management (relationship, upsell, retention) | Knowledge capture (IP creation, training)

### For Platform / Marketplace Businesses:
**Primary Activities:** Supply acquisition (onboarding suppliers/creators) | Platform development (core product, algorithms, matching) | Demand acquisition (user acquisition, activation) | Transaction facilitation (matching, payment, trust, dispute resolution) | Ecosystem management (APIs, partners, developers)

### For SaaS / Software Businesses:
**Primary Activities:** Product development (R&D, engineering, design) | Go-to-market (marketing, sales, partnerships) | Onboarding & activation (implementation, training, time-to-value) | Customer success (adoption, expansion, retention) | Platform & infrastructure (hosting, security, reliability)

**Support Activities:** Technology & data infrastructure | Talent & HR | Procurement | Finance, legal, corporate admin

For each activity, assess: Performance level (best-in-class / competitive / below par / broken) | Cost as % of revenue | Strategic importance (does it drive competitive advantage?)

## Step 2: Cost-to-Serve Modeling (The 80/20)

Estimate the cost of delivering value through each activity. This separates the vital few from the trivial many.

**Cost pools:** For each activity, identify the three cost drivers: headcount, technology/infrastructure, and direct spend. Allocate overhead realistically (not equally).

**Cost-to-serve:** Express costs as % of revenue, $/transaction, or $/customer. Find the 20% of activities consuming 80% of cost — these are your efficiency levers.

**Activity causality:** Which activities drive cost in other activities? Example: poor procurement (support activity) drives cost throughout operations. A 10% improvement in procurement might reduce total cost by 2-3%.

Output: Cost waterfall showing total value chain cost, activity-level breakdown, top cost drivers, and cost per primary activity.

## Step 3: Linkage Analysis — Why Activities Create Defensibility

Activities don't exist in isolation. Strong linkages between activities create defensibility that single-activity analysis misses.

**Example:** Zara's design → manufacturing → distribution linkage. Each activity alone is unremarkable (many competitors design, manufacture, distribute). Together, they create speed-to-market (weeks, not months). No competitor can replicate this by improving a single activity — they'd need to rebuild the entire system. This is defensibility.

**Map linkages:** For each activity pair, is there a reinforcing relationship?
- Design → Operations: Does design choice simplify manufacturing? (Tesla's super-rigid unibody frame is harder to design, easier to manufacture.)
- Operations → Logistics: Does manufacturing discipline reduce defects and simplify fulfillment?
- Logistics → Customer service: Does fast delivery reduce support tickets from impatient customers?
- Service → Product development: Does field feedback loop back into design?

**Linkage strength:** Rate each linkage as strong, moderate, or weak. Strong linkages (3+ activities reinforcing each other) create competitive moats. Weak linkages suggest activities are fragmented — competitors can pick off pieces.

## Step 4: Digital Transformation Overlay

For each activity, define "digitized excellence." What does world-class look like in a digital-first version?

**Activity level:** Design (AI/generative design, simulation) | Manufacturing (IoT, predictive maintenance, robots) | Logistics (demand forecasting, autonomous delivery, real-time tracking) | Sales (AI lead scoring, dynamic pricing, personalization) | Service (chatbots for Tier 1, AI diagnostics) | Customer success (predictive churn, usage analytics)

**Automation potential:** Which activities are candidates for significant automation? (High-volume, repeatable, low judgment) Which require human judgment and shouldn't be fully automated? (Design, complex negotiation, relationship building)

**Investment priority:** Which digital initiatives have highest ROI? Focus on activities that are both cost-heavy and have strong linkages to customer experience.

## Step 5: Capability vs. Activity Mapping

**Activity:** What you DO (manufacture, market, distribute, support).
**Capability:** What you're GOOD AT (manufacturing excellence, brand building, logistics optimization).

Map capabilities to activities. Where do your capabilities create advantage? Where are capabilities weak?

**Capability gaps:** Which activities are strategic but where your capability is below par? These are investment opportunities. A manufacturer might be good at operations but weak at go-to-market — investing in sales capability could unlock margin.

## Step 6: Customer Journey Alignment

Which activities drive customer perception? Which customer touchpoints matter most?

**Align internally to externally:** Customer touchpoints → Internal activities that enable them → Where does excellence/failure translate to customer satisfaction/churn?

Example: E-commerce company. Touchpoints: product discovery, checkout, delivery, return. Internal activities: search algorithm (discovery), checkout UX (payment processing), logistics (delivery speed/accuracy), returns ops. If delivery is broken, it doesn't matter if search is excellent.

## Step 7: Multi-Business Value Chains

For companies with multiple value chains (Amazon: AWS, Retail, Advertising):

**Shared vs. dedicated activities:** Which activities are shared across businesses? (Data infrastructure, finance, HR) Which are dedicated? (AWS engineering is different from retail ops)

**Cross-subsidization:** Does AWS fund retail investments? Do retail customers subsidize AWS infrastructure? Map profit flows.

**Cannibalization:** Do businesses compete or complement? Does AWS make retail more efficient, or do they compete for investment?

## Step 8: Benchmarking Framework

**Identify competitors and best-in-class:** Who are your direct competitors? Who is best-in-class in each activity across any industry?

**Activity-level benchmarking:** For each activity, find the external benchmark (cost as % of revenue, speed, quality, unit economics). How do you compare? Where are you ahead? Where are you behind?

**Root cause analysis:** If you're behind, why? Is it a capability gap, an investment gap, or a design-of-the-activity problem?

## Step 9: Outsourcing Decision Framework (Beyond Unit Cost)

Don't just compare internal cost vs. external cost. Use total cost of ownership:

**Direct cost** (what it costs them to do it) + **Coordination cost** (time to manage, integrate, rework) + **Quality risk** (rework, customer impact) + **Speed risk** (can they hit your timeline?) + **Strategic control loss** (you're now dependent)

**Decision rule:** Outsource only if external TCO is significantly lower (not just marginally) AND the activity is not a source of competitive advantage AND switching cost is manageable.

## Step 10: Activity System Map (Visual)

Create a visual showing how activities connect. Activities in the center that many others depend on are leverage points. Activities on the periphery with few connections are candidates for outsourcing.

Example: In a SaaS company, product development connects to most other activities (go-to-market messaging, customer success, platform reliability). It's the leverage point. Procurement connects only to vendor management. It's peripheral.

## Step 11: Worked Example — E-Commerce Fashion Retailer

**Activities:** Merchandising (what to buy) | Buying (negotiating with suppliers) | Supply chain (inventory, warehousing) | E-commerce platform (website, app) | Fulfillment (picking, packing, shipping) | Customer service (returns, support) | Analytics (customer insights).

**Cost waterfall:** Merchandising 5% | Buying 8% | Supply chain 12% | E-commerce 6% | Fulfillment 15% | Customer service 4% | Analytics 2% | Corporate overhead 15% | Vendor/supplier margin 32%.

**Value creation:** Merchandising (trendy assortment) | E-commerce (frictionless UX) | Fulfillment speed (competitive advantage).

**Linkage:** Merchandising → Buying (right items cheaper) → Supply chain (right inventory levels) → Fulfillment (no stockouts, less backlog).

**Biggest lever:** Improve merchandising forecast accuracy by 10% → Reduce excess inventory by 10% → Free up working capital, reduce write-offs, reduce supply chain cost by 2%.

**Digital transformation:** Merchandising (predictive analytics for trend forecasting) | Fulfillment (robotics, route optimization).

**Cost leakage:** Supplier has pricing power (buying is weak). Opportunity: consolidate suppliers, invest in buying capability, or vertically integrate key categories.
