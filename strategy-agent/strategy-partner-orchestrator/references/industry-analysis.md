# Industry & Competitive Analysis (10x Version)

## What Changed from the Original
The original Five Forces was a static snapshot that treated all forces equally, missed profit pools and value migration (the highest-value insights), provided no quantification rubric, ignored force interactions, and had no platform economics module. This version adds profit pool mapping, value migration analysis, quantified 1-5 scoring with decision rules, force interaction matrices, dedicated platform/regulatory modules, industry benchmarks, data source guidance, and worked examples for different industry types.

## When to Use
Use before any market entry, investment, or competitive positioning decision. This replaces generic Five Forces analysis with a living, predictive framework that identifies *where profit actually sits* and *where it's moving*. Pair with Competitive Landscape for a complete picture.

## Step 1: Industry Characterization

Characterize the industry to determine which analytical lens dominates:

**Industry Type:**
- **Traditional/physical** (manufacturing, retail, logistics) → Full Porter's Five Forces applies; focus on cost position and scale
- **Platform/marketplace** (Uber, Airbnb, app stores) → Network effects and multi-sided dynamics dominate; use platform-specific module (Step 6)
- **SaaS/subscription** (Salesforce, Netflix) → Switching costs and customer lifetime value are the structural moat
- **Regulated** (healthcare, banking, utilities) → Regulatory environment is the primary force (Step 7); regulatory risk often outweighs competitive risk
- **Emerging/nascent** → Five Forces are premature; focus on market formation and standards battles instead

**Lifecycle Stage:**
- **Emerging** → Technology risk and winner-definition matter most; margin analysis is premature
- **Growth** → Share capture and scale advantages matter most; margins vary wildly
- **Mature** → Cost position and margin sustainability matter most; consolidation dynamics
- **Declining** → Exit barriers and harvest vs. divest strategies matter most

## Step 2: Profit Pool & Value Migration Analysis

This is where textbook Five Forces fails. Before rating forces, map where money actually sits:

**Profit Pool Mapping:**
- Map each step in the value chain: raw materials → production → distribution → retail → customer support
- Estimate the $ margin at each step (use industry reports, 10-K filings, interviews)
- Identify which steps are high-margin (often near customer, brand, or unique IP) vs. low-margin (commodity steps)
- The forces matter *most* where margins are highest

**Value Migration Patterns (Next 3-5 Years):**
- Is profit shifting toward suppliers (e.g., cloud shifting margin from software vendors to infrastructure)? → Entry threat weakens, supplier power strengthens
- Is profit shifting toward distribution/customers (e.g., retailers gaining power over CPG brands)? → Buyer power strengthens
- Is profit consolidating (fewer, larger winners)? → Rivalry intensity increases; entry barriers rise
- Is profit moving to complements (e.g., app stores capturing more than app developers)? → Ecosystem control becomes dominant

**Data sources:** 10-K gross margins by segment, industry analyst reports (Gartner, IDC), benchmarks from financial databases (Pitchbook, Capital IQ), customer interviews on willingness to pay.

## Step 3: Quantified Force Scoring (1-5 Rubric)

Replace vague Favorable/Unfavorable with numeric scores. Rate each force 1-5 from the incumbent's perspective (1 = highly attractive, 5 = highly unattractive).

**Competitive Rivalry** (1 = fragmented, many winners possible; 5 = brutal, winner-take-all)
- 1: >20 competitors, high differentiation, 15%+ combined annual growth, low fixed costs, high switching costs
- 2: 10-20 competitors, some differentiation, 8-15% growth, moderate fixed costs
- 3: 5-10 competitors, modest differentiation, 3-8% growth, mixed fixed cost structures
- 4: 3-5 competitors, commoditized, <3% growth, high fixed costs, low switching costs
- 5: 1-2 major competitors, winner-take-most dynamics, negative growth, extreme fixed costs, or perfect substitutes

**Buyer Power** (1 = fragmented, low leverage; 5 = concentrated, extreme leverage)
- 1: >1000 buyers, each <0.5% of revenue, high switching costs, low price transparency, no backward integration threat
- 2: 100-1000 buyers, each <2% of revenue, moderate switching costs, some transparency
- 3: 20-100 buyers, each 2-5% of revenue, switching costs moderate-low, growing price transparency
- 4: 5-20 buyers, each 5-15% of revenue, low switching costs, high price transparency, backward integration feasible
- 5: <5 buyers, each >15% of revenue, zero switching costs, perfect price transparency, backward integration active or credible

**Supplier Power** (1 = fragmented, low leverage; 5 = concentrated, extreme leverage)
- 1: >100 suppliers, perfect substitutes, you are 0.5-1% of their revenue, zero lock-in, zero forward integration threat
- 2: 20-100 suppliers, good alternatives, you are 1-3% of their revenue, moderate lock-in
- 3: 10-20 suppliers, some alternatives, you are 3-10% of their revenue, moderate lock-in, some forward integration threat
- 4: 3-10 suppliers, few alternatives, you are 10-30% of their revenue, high switching costs, forward integration feasible
- 5: 1-3 suppliers, no alternatives, you are >30% of their revenue, total lock-in, forward integration active or imminent

**Threat of Entry** (1 = high barriers, difficult to enter; 5 = low barriers, easy entry)
- 1: >$1B capital required, massive brand/scale advantage, regulated/licensed, strong network effects, proprietary technology, high switching costs
- 2: $100M-1B capital required, significant brand/scale, some barriers, weak network effects
- 3: $10M-100M capital required, moderate brand advantage, mixed barriers, limited moats
- 4: <$10M capital required, no meaningful brand advantage, light regulation, weak moats, easy distribution access
- 5: <$1M capital required, zero barriers, open market, no IP, free distribution, immediate substitution available

**Threat of Substitution** (1 = few alternatives, high switching cost; 5 = many alternatives, zero switching cost)
- 1: No meaningful substitutes, solving unique problem, high switching cost (technical, contractual, relationship), non-discretionary spend
- 2: Few substitutes, moderate differentiation, high-moderate switching cost, mostly non-discretionary
- 3: Some substitutes, moderate differentiation, moderate switching cost, mix of discretionary/non-discretionary
- 4: Many substitutes, low differentiation, low switching cost, largely discretionary spend
- 5: Perfect substitutes or non-consumption, zero differentiation, zero switching cost, easily replaceable, highly discretionary

**Ecosystem/Complements** (1 = strong, controlled by you; 5 = weak, controlled by others)
- 1: Few/no critical complements needed, you control ecosystem, high lock-in, integrations proprietary
- 2: Some complements matter, you control most, moderate lock-in, some proprietary integrations
- 3: Complements important but mixed control, open integrations, moderate interdependence
- 4: Many critical complements, you control few, open ecosystem, low lock-in, high interdependence
- 5: Your product is worthless without complements, you control none, extreme platform power loss, parasitic relationship

## Step 4: Force Interaction Effects

Forces don't operate independently. Identify which force combinations create leverage:

**High-Impact Interactions:**
- High buyer power + high supplier power → Industry caught in the middle; crush margins (retail, automotive parts distribution)
- High rivalry + low entry barriers + high substitution → Race to zero; avoid (most commodities)
- Low rivalry + high switching costs + strong ecosystem → Sustainable high-margin moat (enterprise software, utilities)
- High entry barriers + low buyer power + moderate rivalry → Stable, profitable position (regulated utilities, specialized manufacturing)

**Use this to adjust your final attractiveness rating:** If forces are balanced, industries are often stable. If two or more forces are misaligned against you (both scoring 4-5), the industry is treacherous.

## Step 5: Industry-Specific Benchmarks

Know what "good" margins look like for your industry type:

**Traditional Manufacturing:** 8-15% EBITDA margin, 4-8% net margin. Exceptions: branded/differentiated (15-25%), or extreme scale (2-4%)

**Retail:** 1-4% net margin. Luxury retail: 8-15%. Exceptions: high-velocity, low-shrink formats

**SaaS (recurring):** 60-85% gross margin. 10-40% EBITDA margin at scale. Early stage: -30% to 0%

**Marketplaces/Platforms:** 40-60% gross margin on transaction fees. 0-30% EBITDA. Scaling phase: negative or break-even

**Enterprise Services:** 30-50% gross margin. 10-25% EBITDA. Highly variable by labor arbitrage and structure

**Use this to calibrate:** If the industry benchmarks show 12% typical margins but you're seeing 25%, either (a) the leaders have a genuine moat, or (b) margin compression is coming.

## Step 6: Platform & Marketplace-Specific Module

If you classified the industry as Platform/Marketplace in Step 1, replace traditional Five Forces with platform economics:

**Cross-Side Network Effects:**
- How much does adding a supplier increase demand from buyers, and vice versa?
- Strength (1-5): 1 = weak (both sides value the platform modestly), 5 = extreme (platform is the only option for matching)
- **Critical question:** Is the platform a "must-have" meeting place or a "nice-to-have" convenience?

**Chicken-and-Egg Problem:**
- Does the platform need critical mass on one side before the other side joins? (Uber needed drivers before riders; still a factor)
- Severity (1-5): 1 = solved (one side is abundant), 5 = unsolved (requires heavy subsidy or dual-sided launch)

**Multi-Homing:**
- What percentage of users operate on multiple competing platforms simultaneously? (High multi-homing = low switching costs = high rivalry)
- Rate (1-5): 1 = exclusive (users on one platform only), 5 = universal (users on all platforms equally)

**Platform Envelopment Risk:**
- Can a larger player (Google, Amazon, Facebook) absorb this platform as a feature?
- Risk (1-5): 1 = low (core business differs, integration adds little value), 5 = high (natural fit, Google/Amazon could own it tomorrow)

**Data Advantages & Defensibility:**
- Does controlling the platform give unique data that competitors can't replicate?
- Defensibility (1-5): 1 = none (data is public or easily replicated), 5 = extreme (proprietary data creates competitive advantages)

**Scoring the platform:** Average the five scores above. <2 = extremely attractive, defensible platform. 3-4 = competitive dynamics will pressure margins. >4 = expect brutal competition and eventual consolidation.

## Step 7: Regulatory Environment Module

For any regulated industry, regulatory environment *is* the primary force, not a secondary consideration:

**Regulatory Landscape:**
- **Stability:** Is regulation stable, in flux, or openly hostile? (1 = very stable; 5 = chaotic, frequent upheaval)
- **Barriers created:** Does regulation keep competitors out (e.g., banking licenses, pharmaceutical approval)? (1 = low barriers; 5 = extreme barriers)
- **Margin control:** Does regulation cap margins, mandate pricing, or dictate cost structures? (1 = no control; 5 = complete control)
- **Compliance cost:** What's the annual compliance cost as % of revenue? <2% is manageable. >10% is a structural headwind.

**Regulatory Risk Vectors:**
- What specific regulatory changes would destroy the business model? (Identify 2-3 credible scenarios)
- What's the probability of those changes in the next 3-5 years? (Use expert interviews, regulatory tracking, historical frequency)
- What's the financial impact if they occur? (Can you absorb it, or does it kill profitability?)

**Use regulatory analysis to adjust industry attractiveness:** Even if Five Forces looks great, regulatory risk of 4-5 (high instability + high margin control) can make an industry unattractive.

## Step 7B: Influence & Advocacy Ecosystem Analysis

For industries where adoption is driven by expert influence rather than (or in addition to) direct sales — healthcare, enterprise software, regulated markets, professional services:

**Key Opinion Leader (KOL) Mapping:**
- Who influences adoption decisions? What is their relationship with each player?
- Are KOL relationships contractual (advisory boards, consulting), informal (conference networks), or institutional (academic appointments)?
- Score KOL concentration: 1 (KOLs irrelevant to adoption) to 5 (adoption entirely KOL-driven, e.g., pharmaceutical prescribing patterns)

**Press & Media Advocacy:**
- Which products/companies have favorable media coverage? What narrative momentum exists?
- Is media advocacy creating demand pull that doesn't show up in market share data yet?
- Score: 1 (media irrelevant) to 5 (media/press drives adoption, e.g., consumer health, fintech)

**Professional Association & Standards Alignment:**
- Which industry bodies, standards organizations, or guideline committees favor which approaches?
- Are clinical guidelines, industry standards, or best-practice recommendations creating structural advantages?
- Score: 1 (no standards influence) to 5 (standards/guidelines dictate adoption, e.g., pharmaceutical treatment guidelines)

**Advocacy Asymmetry Assessment:**
- Does one competitor have disproportionate influence access that doesn't show up in market share data?
- Could a smaller player with stronger KOL relationships outperform a larger player with weaker advocacy networks?
- This is often the most underestimated force in regulated and professional industries.

**Use advocacy analysis to adjust competitive assessment:** In KOL-driven industries (score 4-5), advocacy networks can be more predictive of future market share than current sales data. Factor advocacy asymmetry into competitive positioning and M&A target evaluation.

## Step 8: Data Sources & How to Find Industry Insights

Don't guess. Use real data:

**Public Filings:**
- 10-K/10-Q (US), Annual Reports (global) → Segment margins, revenue mix, management commentary on competition
- Investor presentations → Forward guidance, competitive positioning, strategic bets
- S-1 filings → Immature markets, emerging leaders, margin trajectories

**Industry Reports:**
- Gartner, IDC, McKinsey, BCG → Market sizing, growth rates, competitive positioning
- Industry-specific analysts → Moody's for credit, eSpeed for logistics, etc.
- Trade associations → Rarely neutral, but they have real data (margins, customer satisfaction, consolidation)

**Customer & Expert Interviews:**
- Buyers (2-3 customers) → Switching costs, willingness to pay, alternatives they consider, pain points
- Suppliers (1-2) → Pricing power, forward integration threat, customer concentration
- Former/current employees (1-2) → Competitive intensity, margin trends, strategic priorities
- Analysts and consultants (1) → Macro trends, disruption vectors, accuracy calibration

**Financial Databases:**
- PitchBook, Capital IQ, S&P Global → M&A trends, consolidation patterns, capital intensity
- Benchmark databases → Industry margins, labor costs, capital requirements by segment

**Secondary Intelligence:**
- Company earnings call transcripts → Management's view of competition, disruption, regulatory risk
- Patent databases → Technology differentiation and disruption threats
- Tech blogs, news archives → Disruption signals 12-18 months before they appear in official reports

## Step 9: Worked Example — How Quantification Works Across Industry Types

**Example 1: SaaS Accounting Software (Mature, High-Growth Consolidation)**

*Characterization:* SaaS, growth stage (15% YoY growth), consolidating toward 2-3 leaders (Intuit, Xero, others)

*Profit pool:* License revenue (high margin, 70%), services/support (lower margin, 40%), add-ons (variable)

*Value migration:* Toward ecosystem (integrations with banks, payroll, tax). Intuit controls platform; others depend on Intuit's API goodwill.

*Force scores:*
- Rivalry: 3 (5-7 competitors, moderate differentiation, growth slowing, land-and-expand model favors incumbents)
- Buyer power: 2 (many small/medium businesses, high switching cost once embedded, but price sensitivity on upfront)
- Supplier power: 1 (no critical suppliers, hosting is commodity, labor is available)
- Entry threat: 4 (low capital, but brand/integration advantage favors Intuit and Xero; new entrants must convince customers to rip-and-replace)
- Substitution: 2 (spreadsheets are a weak substitute; banks building in-house solutions are a real threat)
- Ecosystem: 4 (Intuit controls integrations; smaller players are dependent)

*Attractiveness score:* 5/10 (moderate attractiveness). High switching costs protect margins, but ecosystem control by Intuit and consolidation risk threaten profitability for non-leaders.

*Recommendation:* Enter only if you can differentiate on a vertical (e.g., nonprofits, construction) or geographic (emerging markets) angle. Otherwise, expect acquisition or exit.

---

**Example 2: Indian E-Commerce Logistics (Emerging, High Competition)**

*Characterization:* Platform/marketplace, emerging-to-growth stage, brutal competition (Flipkart, Amazon, Delhivery, others), 35% YoY growth.

*Profit pool:* Shipping fees (moderate margin, 25-35%), fulfillment services (low margin, 10-15%), financing/insurance (high margin, but low volume).

*Value migration:* Toward buyers (Amazon, Flipkart control pricing); toward platforms (Delhivery and others trying to become Amazon-independent).

*Force scores:*
- Rivalry: 5 (10+ competitors, zero differentiation except delivery speed, race to coverage and scale, negative unit economics during scaling)
- Buyer power: 4 (top 3 e-commerce platforms control 70% of volume, can dictate pricing, backward integration threat credible)
- Supplier power: 2 (pickup agents and delivery agents are fragmented, low switching cost)
- Entry threat: 4 (capital required ($50M-500M depending on geography), but no durable moat; Amazon/Flipkart can absorb logistics)
- Substitution: 3 (international logistics, cash-on-delivery, pickup points are alternatives; delivery expectations rising)
- Ecosystem: 4 (logistics networks depend on buyer platforms; little ecosystem control; envelopment risk from buyers)

*Attractiveness score:* 3/10 (unattractive). High rivalry, buyer power, and envelopment risk make profitability very difficult outside top 2-3 players.

*Recommendation:* Only enter if you have a specific geographic or operational moat (e.g., rural logistics) that larger players can't replicate. Expect consolidation; position for acquisition or retreat.
