# Competitive Landscape Analysis (10/10 Strategic Playbook)

## What Changed
Added six missing layers: intelligence gathering methodology (where to find real competitive data), capability assessment frameworks (beyond positioning), financial benchmarking that produces insights, structured war gaming for strategic thinking, pre-built response playbooks for competitor moves, and win/loss integration to ground analysis in actual customer decisions. The framework now works for both data-rich (public) and data-poor (private/stealth) competitors.

## When to Use
Use when you need to understand not just competitor positioning but their actual capabilities, constraints, and likely responses to your moves. Pair with Industry Analysis for structural context. Critical before major strategic commitments (pricing changes, product pivots, market entry, M&A).

## Step 1: Intelligence Gathering (Data Sources)

Competitive advantage is 70% data quality, 30% analysis. Know where to look:

**SEC Filings (Public Companies)**
- 10-K: Revenue breakdown by segment, R&D spend, customer concentration, risk disclosures
- 10-Q: Quarterly momentum, executive commentary on competition, capital allocation
- S-1: Growth rates, unit economics (if included), GTM strategy

**Organizational Capability Signals**
- LinkedIn hiring patterns: What roles? Where? Growth rate? What skills missing?
- Job postings: Reveals product direction, technology gaps, team expansion
- Conference talks: Executive messaging, technical roadmap hints, culture signals
- Patent filings: Technology direction, 3-5 year innovation themes

**Customer Intelligence**
- G2/Capterra reviews: Win/loss language, actual use cases, why customers switch
- Glassdoor/blind: Employee sentiment on strategy, technical quality, burnout (culture = execution capacity)
- Social media sentiment: Reddit, Twitter, HN — unfiltered customer frustration
- Customer interviews: Direct win/loss reasons if you can access (via sales, industry relationships)

**Financial & Market Data**
- Crunchbase funding: Burn rate, runway implications, investor thesis
- App Store rankings: User growth, retention proxy, feature adoption pace
- Website traffic (SimilarWeb, Semrush): Growth rate, geographic focus, channel mix
- Pricing pages: Models, tier positioning, price changes (test for elasticity)

**Digital Competitive Signals**
- SEO keyword battles: Who's winning what keywords? Implies positioning + customer intent
- Podcast/podcast sponsorships: Audience targeting, budget allocation, brand strategy
- Ad spend monitoring (SpyFu, Semrush): Category focus, messaging rotation
- Email signup trends: Newsletter growth rate as engagement proxy

## Step 2: Capability Assessment (Beyond Positioning)

Positioning tells you where they are. Capability tells you where they can go.

**Organizational Capabilities**
- Engineering depth: Architecture patterns, hiring seniority, technical debt visible in product
- Product velocity: Feature release cadence, complexity of releases, iteration speed
- GTM effectiveness: CAC, LTV, churn trends (visible in 10-K), sales efficiency (revenue per headcount)
- Capital capacity: Runway, burn rate, access to markets (public = cheaper capital)

**Technical Capabilities**
- Technology stack (Stack Overflow, GitHub, job postings): Scalability limits, modernization debt
- API/integration approach: Extensibility, lock-in strategy
- Data infrastructure: Implications for AI, personalization, competitive moat
- Security/compliance posture: Ability to compete upmarket or in regulated verticals

**Market Capabilities**
- Distribution relationships: Partnerships, channel presence, sales network density
- Brand strength: Net Promoter signals, brand recall, switching costs
- Customer relationships: Concentration risk, contract lengths, expansion potential
- International presence: Requires different capabilities per market

## Step 3: Financial Benchmarking (Analysis, Not Just Data)

Numbers only matter if they produce strategic insight.

**Revenue Metrics**
- Growth rate, quarterly trajectory, market share trends
- Revenue per employee: Efficiency proxy. Public SaaS: $75K-$150K range
- Customer acquisition cost (CAC) to lifetime value (LTV) ratio: Unit economics health
- Net revenue retention: Expansion potential, customer stickiness

**Profitability Analysis**
- Gross margin: If lower, they're more price-competitive or have tech disadvantage
- Operating margin: Business model sustainability, reinvestment capacity
- R&D as % of revenue: 15-30% indicates growth investment; <10% suggests mature/cost mode
- Sales & marketing as % of revenue: Channel efficiency, market position confidence

**Capital Efficiency**
- Burn rate + runway: Time to profitability, desperation indicators
- Return on invested capital: Is growth expensive or organic?
- Asset-light vs. asset-heavy: Long-term margin potential

## Step 4: Competitive War Gaming

Play the game from their chair. This is not observation; it's strategic thinking.

**For each top 3 competitor, answer:**
- **If we launch [feature], what's their most damaging response?** (Not their best response — their fastest, cheapest, most leveraging)
- **What are their strategic constraints?** Contractual obligations, technical debt, customer base expectations, board mandates
- **What moves do they want to make but can't?** (This reveals where your freedom of action exceeds theirs)
- **What's their cost of competing with us vs. their cost of ignoring us?** Where will they fight hardest?
- **If they lose 20% of their top customers in Q2, what do they do?** (Stress test their options)

**Build a 12-month response playbook for each:**
| Our Move | Their Likely Response | Timing | Countermeasure |
|----------|---------------------|--------|-----------------|
| Price drop 15% | Match or emphasize feature lead | <30 days | Lock in contracts, emphasize switching cost |
| Launch upmarket | Land-and-expand, add enterprise sales | 6-9 months | Win their churn customers with migration support |
| Acquire [Company] | De-risk vertical with partnerships | 3-6 months | Negotiate non-compete, acquire faster |

## Step 5: Win/Loss Integration

Grounding competitive analysis in actual customer decisions.

**Ask your sales team:**
- Why did we lose to [Competitor] this quarter? What was the real reason?
- Why did we win against [Competitor]? What did they fail to do?
- Which competitor do we see most? In which segments?
- What false claims do competitors make that work on customers anyway?

**Pattern recognition:**
- Which competitor wins on price? On speed? On features? On trust?
- Are losses concentrated in specific segments or use cases? (Reveals positioning blind spots)
- Do ex-customers defect to the same competitor? (Indicates functional weakness vs. sales loss)

**Update your threat ranking quarterly** — actual customer behavior beats theory every time.

## Step 6: Information Asymmetry Playbook

Competitors are private = data is scarce. Here's how to infer reality.

**Earnings calls**: Even private companies' customers publish earnings. Listen for how many mention switching to/from your competitor. Gives you customer-level confidence.

**Hiring analysis over time**: If a private competitor hired 30 engineers in Q1 but only 5 in Q3, something changed. Funding dried up? Pivot? Pause?

**Pricing changes**: Price increases suggest confidence/moat. Price decreases suggest trouble or market share desperation.

**Customer churn signals**: You can't see their churn, but you can see your customers' churn to them. Trends compound.

**Assumption-based reasoning**: Build a scenario tree. "If they don't have X capability, then they can only pursue strategy Y, which means they'll look vulnerable when Z happens."

## Step 7: Narrative Analysis

How competitors position themselves vs. how customers perceive them. The gap is where strategy lives.

**Their narrative**: What do they claim? (Website, earnings, sales pitch)
**Customer narrative**: What do actual customers say? (Reviews, win/loss reasons, social media)
**The gap**: Where they brag about something customers don't care about = budget wasted on wrong positioning

Example: Competitor claims "AI-powered" everywhere (their narrative) but customers care about "less manual data entry" (customer narrative). Implication: Their product doesn't deliver on the real problem — attack on usability, not AI.

## Step 8: Positioning Map (Now Informed)

Only after understanding capabilities, financials, war gaming, and customer perception:

**Dimension selection rule:** Include dimensions where (1) competitors genuinely differ, (2) customers make decisions, and (3) positions are shifting.

**Avoid:** Generic features, hygiene factors, subjective quality without proof.

**Best dimensions:**
- Price vs. complexity (budget constraints vs. feature depth)
- Breadth vs. depth (platform vs. point solution)
- Self-serve vs. sales (GTM model reveals cost structure)
- Speed to value vs. customization (implementation philosophy)

## Step 9: White Space & Opportunity Assessment

Unoccupied positions exist for two reasons: unsolved customer need or unviable economics. Know which.

**Validation questions:**
- Is there demand (willingness to pay) or just absence of supply?
- Can we build it faster/cheaper than competitors can copy it?
- How long until a well-funded competitor enters? (Determines value of first-mover advantage)
- Is the white space defensible or just empty until someone figures out the business model?
