# Business Case Builder

The goal is to build rigorous financial business cases for capital allocation decisions — whether to invest in a project, launch a new product, enter a market, or acquire a company. A business case must prove not just that an initiative will generate returns, but that the returns justify the investment.

---

## 1. Business Case Framework

Every business case answers these questions:

1. **What is the initiative and what does it cost?** (Total investment, timing)
2. **What financial benefits will it generate?** (Revenue, margin improvement, cost savings)
3. **What is the return on investment?** (NPV, IRR, payback period)
4. **How certain are we?** (Sensitivity to key assumptions, scenarios)
5. **Is it the best use of capital?** (Compared to alternatives)

### Step 3: Calculate Net Present Value (NPV)

NPV tells you how much economic value the initiative creates in today's dollars.

```
NPV CALCULATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════

Required inputs:
  – Cash flows (benefits minus costs) for each year
  – Discount rate (what rate of return are we comparing to?)
    Typical: 10-15% for core business, 20-25% for growth initiatives, 30%+ for very risky projects
  – Time horizon (usually 5 years)

Formula:
  NPV = [CF₁/(1+r)¹] + [CF₂/(1+r)²] + [CF₃/(1+r)³] + [CF₄/(1+r)⁴] + [CF₅/(1+r)⁵]
  where:
    CF = Cash flow in year X
    r = Discount rate

Example (5-year projection):
           Year 0    Year 1    Year 2    Year 3    Year 4    Year 5
Investment $(XXM)
Revenue                $XXM      $XXM      $XXM      $XXM      $XXM
Costs                 ($XXM)    ($XXM)    ($XXM)    ($XXM)    ($XXM)
Net Cash Flow $(XXM)   $XXM      $XXM      $XXM      $XXM      $XXM

Discount Factor (@ 12%)
Year 0:     1.000
Year 1:     0.893
Year 2:     0.797
Year 3:     0.712
Year 4:     0.636
Year 5:     0.567

PV of Cash Flows:
Year 0: $(XXM) × 1.000 = $(XXM)
Year 1:  $XXM  × 0.893 = $XXM
Year 2:  $XXM  × 0.797 = $XXM
Year 3:  $XXM  × 0.712 = $XXM
Year 4:  $XXM  × 0.636 = $XXM
Year 5:  $XXM  × 0.567 = $XXM
────────────────────────────────
NPV =                  $XXM

Interpretation:
  – NPV > 0: Initiative creates value (present value of benefits > present value of costs)
  – NPV = 0: Initiative breaks even (returns exactly equal discount rate)
  – NPV < 0: Initiative destroys value (avoid unless strategic)
```

### Step 4: Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

IRR is the discount rate at which NPV = 0. It tells you the percentage return on your investment.

```
IRR CALCULATION
═══════════════════════════════════════════

Formula: Solve for r where NPV = 0
  0 = [CF₁/(1+r)¹] + [CF₂/(1+r)²] + ... + [CF₅/(1+r)⁵] – Initial Investment

Example:
If Year 0 investment = $10M and annual cash flows = $3M for 5 years:
  IRR ≈ 15% (meaning this investment returns 15% annually)

Decision rule:
  IRR > Required rate of return → Accept project
  IRR < Required rate of return → Reject project

  Example: If we require 12% return and IRR = 15%, accept it.
           If we require 12% return and IRR = 10%, reject it.

Comparison:
           Project A   Project B   Project C
IRR        25%        15%         12%
NPV (@ 12% discount) $5M    $2M    $0.2M

If we can fund all three: Rank by NPV in absolute terms, not IRR.
If we can fund only one:  All three have positive NPV; Project A has highest return.
```

### Step 5: Payback Period & Cash Payback

How long until cumulative cash inflows recover the initial investment?

```
PAYBACK PERIOD ANALYSIS
═══════════════════════════════════════════

Cumulative cash flow approach:
           Year 0    Year 1    Year 2    Year 3    Year 4    Year 5
Investment $(XXM)
Net CF              $XXM      $XXM      $XXM      $XXM      $XXM
Cumulative $(XXM)   $(XXM)    $(XXM)    $XXM      $XXM      $XXM

Payback occurs in Year 3 (somewhere between end of Year 2 and Year 3).

Linear interpolation:
  Cumulative at end of Year 2: $(XXM)
  Cash flow in Year 3: $XXM
  Months to payback: [Cumulative deficit / Annual cash flow] × 12 months
  = X.X months into Year 3
  = Year 2 + X.X/12 years
  = X.X years total

Interpretation:
  – Shorter payback = Lower risk (recover investment faster)
  – Longer payback = Higher risk (takes longer to recover investment)
  – Typical threshold: For growth initiatives, payback < 3 years is good
```

## 2. Risk-Adjusted Returns

Not all returns are equally likely. Adjust returns for uncertainty.

### Step 1: Scenario-Based Returns

Build three return scenarios:

```
SCENARIO ANALYSIS: RETURNS
═══════════════════════════════════════════

BASE CASE (Most Likely)
  Key assumptions: [List assumptions used in base case]
  NPV: $XXM
  IRR: X%
  Payback: X years
  Probability: 50% (what's the likelihood of this outcome?)

UPSIDE CASE (Things Go Right)
  Changes from base:
    – Revenue is X% higher (faster adoption, larger deal sizes, etc.)
    – Costs are Y% lower (efficiency gains, no overruns)
  NPV: $XXM (much higher)
  IRR: X%
  Payback: X years (faster)
  Probability: 25% (what's the upside probability?)

DOWNSIDE CASE (Things Go Wrong)
  Changes from base:
    – Revenue is X% lower (slower adoption, market saturation, competition)
    – Costs are Y% higher (overruns, scope creep, team inefficiency)
  NPV: $XXM or $(XXM) [could be negative]
  IRR: X% or negative
  Payback: X years or never (breaks down financially)
  Probability: 25% (what's the downside probability?)

EXPECTED VALUE (Probability-Weighted)
  Expected NPV = (Base NPV × 50%) + (Upside NPV × 25%) + (Downside NPV × 25%)
  Expected IRR = (Base IRR × 50%) + (Upside IRR × 25%) + (Downside IRR × 25%)

This gives a more realistic view of returns given uncertainty.
```

### Step 2: Sensitivity Analysis

Which assumptions have the biggest impact on returns?

```
SENSITIVITY: IMPACT ON NPV
═══════════════════════════════════════════

For each key assumption, test ±10%, ±20%, ±30% changes:

Assumption: Revenue adoption rate
  Base case: X% of target market by Year 3
  NPV range:
    –20% (slower adoption):        NPV = $XXM (still positive but lower)
    Base case:                     NPV = $XXM
    +20% (faster adoption):        NPV = $XXM (significantly higher)
  Sensitivity: For every 1% change in adoption rate, NPV changes by $XXM

Assumption: Implementation cost
  Base case: $XXM (includes people, systems, vendor fees)
  NPV range:
    +30% (cost overruns):          NPV = $XXM (still positive?)
    Base case:                     NPV = $XXM
    –30% (execution efficiency):   NPV = $XXM
  Sensitivity: For every $1M overage, NPV declines by $XXM

Assumption: Time to full benefit realization
  Base case: Benefits fully realized in Year 3
  NPV range:
    Year 2 full realization:       NPV = $XXM (faster payback, higher NPV)
    Year 3 (base):                 NPV = $XXM
    Year 4 (delayed):              NPV = $XXM (slower payback, lower NPV)
  Sensitivity: For every year of delay, NPV declines by $XXM

SENSITIVITY RANKING
Which 3 assumptions have the biggest impact on NPV?
  1. [Assumption] — impacts NPV by $XXM if ±20%
  2. [Assumption] — impacts NPV by $XXM if ±20%
  3. [Assumption] — impacts NPV by $XXM if ±20%

Decision: We should focus most on validating these three assumptions.
```

### Step 3: Break-Even Analysis

At what point does the initiative break even financially?

```
BREAK-EVEN ANALYSIS
═══════════════════════════════════════════

The initiative breaks even (NPV = 0) when:
  [What would have to be true? Lower adoption? Higher costs?]

Minimum revenue needed:
  Total investment: $XXM
  Cost of capital: 12% annually
  If benefits spread over 5 years, required annual benefit = $XXM
  vs. forecasted annual benefit = $XXM
  Buffer: X% (if benefits decline by more than X%, we're underwater)

Maximum allowable cost:
  Projected benefits: $XXM (NPV at 12%)
  Maximum investment to achieve NPV = $0: $XXM
  Current proposed investment: $XXM
  Budget cushion: $XXM (investment can overrun by $XXM and we break even)

If any of these "breaks are tight" (< 10% buffer), project is risky.
```

## 3. Stage-Gate Investment Framework

For multi-phase projects, use stage gates to limit risk and preserve optionality.

```
STAGE-GATE INVESTMENT FRAMEWORK
═══════════════════════════════════════════

STAGE 1: DISCOVERY & VALIDATION (Cost: $XXM)
Timeline: 3 months
Objectives:
  ☐ Validate product concept with customers
  ☐ Confirm addressable market is as large as assumed
  ☐ Confirm internal capability to execute
  ☐ Refine financial assumptions
Investment: $XXM (small, low-risk)
Gate Decision:
  ☑ PROCEED to Stage 2 if [conditions met]
  ☐ PIVOT if [conditions not met]
  ☐ KILL if [evidence suggests wrong market / wrong approach]

STAGE 2: PILOT / MVP (Cost: $XXM)
Timeline: 6 months
Objectives:
  ☐ Build MVP and launch with early customers
  ☐ Validate product-market fit (NPS, retention, willingness to pay)
  ☐ Validate unit economics (actual CAC, actual churn, etc.)
  ☐ Refine go-to-market approach
Investment: $XXM (medium)
Gate Decision:
  ☑ PROCEED to Stage 3 if [conditions met: MVP is X% feature-complete, NPS > Y, customer feedback is positive]
  ☐ ITERATE in Stage 2 if [close but not quite ready; extend timeline]
  ☐ KILL if [product-market fit not evident; churn is too high; customers not adopting]

STAGE 3: SCALE & COMMERCIALIZATION (Cost: $XXM)
Timeline: 12 months
Objectives:
  ☐ Scale GTM (sales, marketing, distribution)
  ☐ Expand to new customer segments / geographies
  ☐ Build customer success / retention motion
  ☐ Achieve profitability / positive unit economics
Investment: $XXM (large)
Gate Decision:
  ☑ PROCEED to Stage 4 if [scale targets hit: XXM ARR, NPS X, CAC payback < Y months]
  ☐ OPTIMIZE in Stage 3 if [on track but needs more time; extend investment]
  ☐ EXIT if [economics don't work; churn is too high; market not absorbing product]

STAGE 4: MATURE / HARVEST (Ongoing)
Timeline: Ongoing
Objectives:
  ☐ Optimize margins and operating leverage
  ☐ Expand market share in existing segments
  ☐ Explore new use cases and adjacencies
Investment: Limited (primarily OpEx to support growth)

KEY PRINCIPLE: Each stage limits capital at risk. Don't fund full $XXM
Stage 3 investment until Stage 2 validates the model.
This preserves capital and limits downside if assumptions prove wrong.
```

### Full Probability Chain Rule (Required for Staged R&D / Pipeline Investments)

**Principle:** When evaluating whether to invest in a specific stage of a multi-stage pipeline (R&D, clinical trials, product development), the success probability calculation MUST include ALL upstream stage probabilities — not just the stage being evaluated.

**Why this matters:** Isolating a single stage overstates the probability of reaching commercialization and value realization. The investment decision depends on *cumulative* probability from current state to revenue. Failing to chain probabilities leads to systematically overvaluing pipeline investments and underestimating the required success rate at any given stage.

**Methodology:**
```
P(commercial success) = P(Stage N) × P(Stage N+1) × ... × P(Approval) × P(Commercial Success)

The required success rate at any single stage depends on ALL other stages in the chain.
```

**Worked Example (Pharmaceutical R&D):**

Question: "What Phase II success rate makes a $150M investment worthwhile?"

**WRONG approach (isolated stage):**
- Look at Phase II probability in isolation
- Solve for breakeven: P(Phase II) = 67.8%
- Conclusion: "Phase II needs ~68% probability" ← INCORRECT

**RIGHT approach (full probability chain):**
- Map the FULL chain from current state to revenue:
  - Phase I success probability: 70% (given or industry average)
  - Phase II success probability: ? (this is what we're solving for)
  - Phase III success probability: 50%
  - Regulatory approval probability: 85%
  - Commercial success probability: 90%
- Full chain: 0.70 × P(Phase II) × 0.50 × 0.85 × 0.90 = required threshold
- Solving for breakeven: P(Phase II) must reach approximately **80%** (not 67.8%)
- The 12 percentage point difference represents $18M+ in expected value — enough to change the investment decision

**Anti-pattern:** Never evaluate a stage-gate investment by isolating a single stage probability. Always compute the full chain from current state to value realization. This applies to:
- Pharmaceutical clinical trials (Phase I → II → III → Approval → Commercial)
- Technology R&D pipelines (Prototype → Alpha → Beta → Launch → Scale)
- Market entry sequences (Pilot → Regional → National → International)
- Any investment where value realization requires passing through multiple conditional gates

**Implementation rule:** When building stage-gate investment models, always include a "Full Probability Chain" row that multiplies all stage probabilities together. Present both the isolated stage probability AND the chain probability to decision-makers. If the gap between them is >10 percentage points, flag it as material to the investment decision.

## 5. Common Business Case Failures

Learn to recognize flawed business cases before they get approved:

```
RED FLAGS IN BUSINESS CASES
═══════════════════════════════════════════

❌ Vague Benefits Definition
   "This initiative will improve customer satisfaction and reduce costs"
   → Requires: Specific metrics, dollar amounts, timeline

❌ Unrealistic Revenue Ramp
   "We'll acquire 1,000 customers in Year 1 at $10K ACV"
   → Requires: How? CAC is $XXX. Time to close is X months. You can only acquire Y customers in Year 1.

❌ Ignore Implementation Cost Overruns
   "Implementation will cost $XXM" (no buffer for delays, scope creep)
   → Requires: History of project overruns in your company. Budget +25% contingency minimum.

❌ Single Point Forecast
   "Revenue will be $XXM" (no ranges, no scenarios)
   → Requires: Base/bull/bear cases. Sensitivity to key assumptions.

❌ Don't Forget Ongoing Costs
   "Initial investment $XXM" (but ignore annual OpEx required to maintain/support)
   → Requires: Full 5-year cash flow including ongoing costs, not just initial investment.

❌ Ignore Opportunity Cost
   "This returns 15% annually" (but ignores that capital could return 20% elsewhere)
   → Requires: Ranking of initiatives. Did you fund the highest-return projects first?

❌ No Downside Scenario
   "Everything goes according to plan" (ignores what happens if assumptions miss)
   → Requires: Downside case. What does NPV look like if revenue is 30% lower?

❌ Vague Go-to-Market
   "We'll market it to [target segment]"
   → Requires: Specific GTM strategy, sales model, pricing, customer acquisition approach

If a business case has any of these failures, send it back for revision.
```
